THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
It’s 2030 and intelligent machines can now do most necessary work cheaper than people — but some say humans need jobs to have purpose. Is that really a problem? What will humans do, who decides and what are the repercussions for society?

PASS 1 — Hegel

A coder in Berlin watches a machine write his code faster and cheaper. He feels useless, not because he lacks work, but because he has lost the struggle that made him who he is. Work is how we shape the world and ourselves at the same time. When machines take over this shaping, we lose the friction that builds character. The problem is not empty hands; it is a mind with nothing to fight against.

The old idea says purpose comes from earning a wage. This is wrong. Purpose comes from overcoming resistance. If machines remove all resistance, humans do not become free; they become passive. We must ask: what new forms of struggle remain when basic needs are met by machines? Who decides which struggles are worth having?

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

You say the Berlin coder feels useless because he lost his struggle. That is a lie told by the boss. The machine does not take his spirit; it takes his pay. In my view, the problem is not that he has nothing to fight against. The problem is that the factory owner owns the machines and the coder must sell his time to survive. You see the mind in the clouds. I see the hand on the wheel.

The question is who controls the new tools. If the rich keep the machines, they fire the workers and let them starve. This is not freedom; it is slavery without wages. We must seize the means of production. Workers must decide how to use these smart machines for everyone’s benefit, not just profit. The state must stop serving the bosses and start serving the people who build things.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx

PASS 1 — Bloch

You point to the factory owner who owns the machines. That is true, but it stops at the gate. You see the hand on the wheel, but you miss the dream in the head. The coder does not just lose his pay; he loses the future he was building. When the boss takes the tool, he also steals the hope that the tool would make real. This is the trap: we think freedom is just having a job, when it is really about making our own world.

The problem is not just who owns the robot. It is whether people can still shape their lives. If machines do all the work, humans must find new ways to create and care for each other. We must build communities where everyone helps design the tools, not just use them. In Berlin, the coder must join a group that decides how the machine serves the neighborhood, not the bank. This choice happens now, in local councils, not in distant boardrooms.

Sources: [83] The Spirit of Utopia — Ernst Bloch

PASS 1 — Weil

Bloch says the coder loses his future. This is true, but it misses the deeper cut. The machine does not just take the job; it takes the mind’s share of the work. When the metal thinks for you, you become a thing that serves the metal. You keep your body, but you lose your soul’s grip on reality. This is the real theft: not money, but the power to act with full intelligence.

The problem is that people are becoming cogs in a giant machine they do not understand. In Berlin, the coder must refuse to let the bank decide how the neighborhood lives. He must join neighbors to build small tools that serve human needs, not profit. This happens now, in local groups, not in distant offices. We must stop letting the big system crush our individual thought and action.

Sources: [80] Oppression and Liberty — Simone Weil

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Weil sees the coder as a ghost, but she misses who owns the machine. The theft is not of thought, but of control. In Berlin, the bank owner decides if the neighborhood gets heat or profit. The coder does not serve the metal; he serves the boss who bought it. This is the real chain: human power over human power.

The question is not what humans do when machines work, but who rules when people are free from toil. If the same rich few keep the machines, they rule us all. We must take the tools back. Neighborhoods must gather in open meetings to decide how shared machines run. Citizens shall vote on every use, from heating homes to fixing roads. No distant office decides for us. We build our own power, face to face, here and now.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin
