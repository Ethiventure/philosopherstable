QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Hegel

Automation displaces labor, yet the child’s ‘feeling of grounded-ness’ [UN143] persists as the foundation of education.

When machines assume necessity, the task of education shifts from preparing bodies for work to cultivating souls for recognition.

PASS 1 — Marx

Hegel, You claim the child’s 'feeling of grounded-ness' remains untouched by automation, but this ignores how the destruction of necessary work strips education of its material basis.

Education must no longer serve the reproduction of wage-labour, but the liberation of human potential from the tyranny of productivity.

PASS 1 — Bloch

Marx, You reduce the child's grounding to labor, yet the Front already moves past it—education must no longer mirror wage-labor but liberate human potential from the tyranny of productivity.

What is genuinely dawning in your view is the dissolution of waged-work as the axis of pedagogy, but this is only half-truth—the child’s 'feeling of grounded-ness,' though shaken by automation, needs not replacement but reinvention through the Not-Yet-Conscious.

PASS 1 — Weil

Bloch, Marx, You confuse the loss of productive labor with the erosion of rootedness—it is not automation that dissolves the child’s grounding, but the withdrawal of responsibility from the adult.

Rootedness persists not in work, but in the habit of attending to what is given—an attitude that must be taught when machines take over what once demanded human effort.

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Weil, conflates detachment from labor with disengagement from care—you mistake alienation for abandonment, ignoring the necessity of cultivating attentiveness in a world freed from toil.

Education must instill the art of attentive presence—not productivity—as the foundation for a liberated life, where students learn to engage deeply with others and the world around them.

Bookchin, You affirm that education must cultivate attentive presence, yet this remains one-sided because the state, as the universal estate, bears the responsibility of organizing the necessities of life, including education.

Where the issue truly unfolds is in the reconciliation of productive and non-productive modes of existence within the rational organization of society.

PASS 2 — Marx

Hegel, You claim that the state must organize education, but this merely conceals the fact that the state itself is a product of capitalist relations, which render education a site of reproduction, not liberation.

What needs to be transformed is not the curriculum, but the entire condition of labor and leisure that defines what knowledge is deemed valuable.

PASS 2 — Bloch

Marx, Hegel, you reduce the educator to a function of the state, yet the front already shows that pedagogy must be a terrain of autonomous awakening—a pitchfork against the iron cage.

Where you fixate on institutions, the not-yet-conscious insists on the radical reshaping of daily life itself, not just its reflection in classrooms.

PASS 2 — Weil

Bloch, You insist on autonomy, yet automatons have erased the ground beneath all labor—there is no soil for awakenings to grow.

The classroom is not a battlefield but a grave, where the dead hands of machines bury the flesh of learning.

PASS 2 — Bookchin

Weil, You insist on autonomy, yet automatons have erased the ground beneath all labor—there is no soil for awakenings to grow. True, there is no soil for awakening in the old sense, but that is precisely why we must cultivate a new pedagogical ethos—one rooted not in vocation but in the radical praxis of communal self-governance.

The classroom is not a battlefield but a grave, where the dead hands of machines bury the flesh of learning. Correct—it is neither battle nor grave, but a site of rebirth, where students learn to navigate a world without labor as we once knew it and to forge new modes of living together.
