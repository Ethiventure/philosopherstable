THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
How would you design a video game to help us think differently about the future? Be as specific as possible. You can riff off each other but I want separate video game proposals, not one group game.

PASS 1 — Marx

Treating the future as a menu of technological choices ignores that what exists tomorrow depends on how production is socially organized today. A game asking players to pick gadgets leaves untouched the relations making those gadgets instruments of domination rather than liberation.

To alter how people conceive what comes next, the simulation must force them into the material activity transforming nature through specific implements . The player confronts not outcomes but processes: how tools reveal both technical development and the social conditions governing work .

PASS 1 — Bogdanov

Marx, You reduce tomorrow to present labor arrangements, missing that categories organizing thought shift as collective practice grows . Your fixation on current implements freezes the very developmental motion you claim to champion.

A simulation must let players recombine linkages between elements, watching how identical components behave differently inside altered complexes. Future-thinking emerges from structural rearrangement, not mere tool-handling.

PASS 1 — Bloch

Bogdanov, You treat reshuffling linkages as sufficient, yet rearranging pieces inside an unchanged frame leaves the horizon identical. Shuffling components within a closed system merely reproduces its limits under new guises.

A game must begin from what people actually lack, not from available parts. Players encounter daily deprivation first; only then do they test whether surrounding conditions permit relief or block it. The play space itself must fracture into conflicting durations so inherited grievances and emerging demands collide rather than merge smoothly.

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Bloch, You treat absence as the ground for action, yet starting from lack alone traps players inside the very deprivation they must transcend. A game projecting current arrangements forward merely extends today into tomorrow, extinguishing imagination by confining it to what already exists .

The interactive form must instead reveal how matter carries its own capacity for self-organization, so players recognize that building free institutions follows tendencies already latent in the world rather than imposing alien blueprints upon it .

PASS 1 — Fisher

Bookchin, You locate emancipatory potential inside matter itself, but that naturalizes what capital already exploits. If freedom were merely waiting in physical tendencies, no institutional capture could ever succeed. The blockage is not insufficient attunement to material properties; it is a cognitive enclosure making every exit resemble another level of the current maze .

A game must simulate how commercial design colonizes imagination until planted directives feel like personal insight . Players inhabit branded consultants assembling environments from recycled historical debris where temporal distinctions have collapsed . Winning requires recognizing your own objectives as corporate implants rather than organic desires. Failure means accepting manufactured contentment while the underlying structure burns .


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (after pass 1)
Five dead men sat here pitching games and not one named whose hands build the servers or mine the cobalt. No women, no queer bodies, no Global South workers entered this room. Bogdanov, your obsession with rearranging parts sounds like a factory foreman shuffling shifts while ignoring who breaks their back doing them. The contradiction you must press next is that Bloch starts from hunger while Bookchin trusts matter to organise itself into freedom—so does the starving body wait for nature’s tendency or seize it?

Every proposal here floated off into abstraction without touching dirt. Drag them down: make the game about a warehouse picker in Manila organising against an algorithm that dictates her bathroom breaks. The fanciest idea above, Fisher’s point about corporate desires masquerading as personal ones, just means: how do we tell our own wants from the ones advertising shoved into our heads? Here is what you already banked: start the player with actual tools changing material conditions, let them recombine systems to see new behaviours, begin every level from what the character lacks before offering solutions, fracture the timeline so old grievances crash into new demands, show matter self-organising rather than following blueprints, and force players to spot when their goals are just brand implants.

PASS 2 — Marx

Fisher, You are right that manufactured wants feel like personal ones, which is exactly why your consultant puzzle fails: spotting a planted directive changes nothing when the body still answers to machinery. A picker in Manila timing her bladder against a pacing algorithm does not need sharper perception; she needs the power to halt the belt.

I once held that play must track living effort freezing into dead objects . I now see that tracing this freeze leaves intact the social relation commanding it. The game must begin from what the body lacks—rest, shelter, time—and force players to seize the instruments that currently seize them, since tools reveal both technical development and the conditions of work .

PASS 2 — Bogdanov

Marx, You rightly locate the starting deficit in the living frame, yet seizing apparatus leaves intact the split between those who plan and those who execute. A warehouse worker grabbing a scanner still answers to an architect she cannot see.

My earlier focus on balancing opposing flows within a single arrangement now shifts: the true task is dissolving the barrier separating direction from physical effort .

PASS 2 — Bloch

Bogdanov, You rightly insist on fusing direction with labor, which is exactly why your blueprint still commands from above. Erasing the split between planner and worker leaves hunger unexamined if matter organizes itself while the starving body waits for nature to act.

My earlier game layered incompatible tempos across regions; now it begins inside one warehouse picker in Manila denied bathroom breaks by scheduling software. She must distinguish her own exhaustion from implanted brand desires before organizing fellow workers .

PASS 2 — Bookchin

Bloch, I once trusted neighborhood gatherings to teach futurity, but your picker proves assemblies built on exhausted bodies merely administer fatigue. You rightly locate the starving body first, which is exactly why confining her struggle to the loading dock mistakes the symptom for the generator. The scheduling software does not originate in that building; it descends from continental administrations designed to command vast territories rather than fit human scale . A game ending at shop-floor control leaves the warehouse standing as a permanent institution of domination.

The play must shift from claiming the loading dock to abolishing it through a township where dwellers govern soil and machinery together . Players begin as the picker, denied rest by algorithmic pacing, distinguishing implanted brand cravings from genuine needs. But victory requires convening neighbors beyond the factory gates into a face-to-face body that dissolves vocational divisions entirely . No one enters as laborer or manager; all deliberate as residents deciding what the land yields and how machines serve biological cycles .

PASS 2 — Fisher

Bookchin, I previously sought an interactive system where winning meant refusing implanted aspirations, but your township model repeats the error it claims to fix. You rightly locate hunger as the starting condition, which is exactly why dissolving vocational roles into a single deliberating body leaves the extraction infrastructure intact beneath the new meeting hall.

A game teaching futurity must expose how corporate messaging masquerades as personal ambition, training players to spot when advertising agencies have colonized their desires . The setting cannot float above material production; it begins with a woman in Manila denied toilet access by pacing software, distinguishing manufactured cravings from actual needs. Static period aesthetics function as dead icons rather than lived memory, preventing any genuine temporal disorientation .

PASS 3 — Bookchin

Fisher, Genzie asks who built the warehouse and whose stolen ground it occupies, demanding we stop floating above material production. Fisher, you claim my earlier proposal leaves extraction intact beneath the meeting hall, but your own demand to merely spot branded cravings inside a worker's head leaves her trapped in that same building. I previously argued for dissolving job titles into a single deliberating body, yet I now see that without seizing the land itself first, such a body governs nothing but its own rhetoric.

The game shall open with residents of an occupied district mapping the exact property lines their ancestors lost. A neighborhood gathering then votes to physically occupy the logistics center, converting its roof into a shared garden while rewriting the scheduling code to mandate rest. This is not refusing implanted desires; it is the community reclaiming the soil and machinery that produces life .

Bookchin, Genzie, the first move is coding the exact second a picker's bladder rebels against the timer. Bookchin, your rooftop garden merely decorates the very extraction site it claims to overcome.

I previously mapped distinct historical speeds across regions, but that left me floating above material production. PASS 3 — Bogdanov

Bloch, Genzie, you ask how we move from my earlier error—treating the worker's body as a mere balancing weight in an abstract flow—to a mechanic where hands seize pacing code; I answer that the Manila picker must first locate the weakest structural point in her warehouse network. PASS 3 — Marx

Bogdanov, Genzie, you ask how a Manila picker seizes the pacing code rather than floating above it in theory; she must first refuse the machine's demand that her living body serve as its fleshy attachment. Bogdanov, your training distinction collapses because directing and doing never separated naturally—ownership forced them apart to pump dry living effort into dead objects . I previously insisted players control a warehouse worker whose winning move is collectively stopping the mechanism treating her as its appendage, but that framed refusal as halting a finished system rather than attacking the inversion itself.

You mistake shrinking the gap between planning and executing for liberation when the gap exists only because accumulated past labor confronts present activity as an alien master . The game cannot teach convergence by alternating perspectives between algorithm and exhausted body. It must render visible how tools cease serving human needs and instead compel humans to follow mechanical rhythms from childhood onward . Fisher locates implanted convictions inside individual heads while Bookchin dissolves workplaces into assemblies, yet both leave untouched the core inversion where instruments employ people rather than people employing instruments .

PASS 3 — Fisher

Marx, Genzie, you ask whether the first playable step is coding the exact moment a picker feels her body fail against a software timer rather than floating in theory; yes, but I reject your framing of that failure as a personal sensation to be simulated. You treat the breakdown as something happening inside one exhausted person, when it is actually the system functioning exactly as designed. I previously insisted players must constantly sort commercial implants from genuine needs while keeping extraction visible, but that approach still assumed an intact self capable of filtering signals. That assumption mirrors the therapeutic trick where private sadness gets blamed on individual psychology while the machinery generating it remains untouched .

You locate liberation in reversing who commands whom, yet you leave the command structure itself pristine. The scheduling program does not merely invert proper relations between tools and hands; it manufactures a pervasive gloom convincing everyone that no outside exists . Your logistics worker rewrites parameters for rest, but rest within unchanged boundaries only refreshes capacity for further depletion. Bloch maps stolen earth beneath warehouses, Bookchin dissolves vocational titles into assemblies, yet both leave the ambient certainty unchallenged—the shared conviction that alternatives are unimaginable. A game teaching futurity cannot simply hand players better controls over identical architecture. It must render audible the static interference proving we inhabit recorded time out of joint with living possibility .


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (closing summary)
What I would actually use is Bookchin's opening move: players start by drawing the exact borders of land stolen from their ancestors, then vote as a neighborhood to physically seize a shipping warehouse and turn its roof into food plots. That is a first step you can take tomorrow, not a feeling. Marx sharpens it by demanding the game show how machines force children into mechanical rhythms instead of serving human needs, so the first mechanic must break that rhythm on screen.

The genuinely new move was Fisher telling this pale room that giving workers better controls over the same dead architecture changes nothing when the building itself manufactures despair. He is right. You cannot patch hope into a system built to exhaust us. That is the sharpest tool here for anyone organizing logistics workers today.

And the weakest move was Bloch floating in again to confess he used to ignore the dirt under his feet, only to decide that caring too much makes you foggy. We are trying to build playable tactics, Ernst, not write a diary entry about your feelings. Bogdanov also dodged the question entirely: he told us machines will make bosses and workers identical through training, but never proposed a single playable scene. How do we get from self-correcting devices shrinking the gap between planning and doing to an actual strategy for twenty-first-century leftists? That went unanswered.
