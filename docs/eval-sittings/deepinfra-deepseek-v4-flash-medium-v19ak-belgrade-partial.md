﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Hegel

When the machine absorbs every task, labour ceases to be education; the human being can walk away from it, and with that step the middle term dissolves . What vanishes is not toil but the mediation, the labour that shaped a will against resistance and trained the particular to universality. The child who never confronts a resistant world remains a mere immediate self, uneducated because never negated.

Education must then recover a different negativity: not the machine's force but the discipline of the concept. We teach the child to labour at thinking itself, to hold contradiction, to suffer the slowness of speculative reasoning. The system of needs, once the school of self-activity, gives way to a pedagogy of recognition and ethical formation. What is taught is not skill but the courage to remain dissatisfied, to long for a higher sphere. This is the 'stimulus to go further' , the negative vector that elevates the child from family love to independence. The pedagogy of play, which shields the child from this urge, leaves it satisfied in its condition — a conceit that crowds out substantial interest .

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

You, Hegel, claim that education must recover a different negativity, but this merely conceals the fact that the machine's force has already shattered the old mediation, and with it, the notion of labour as education. The child who never confronts a resistant world may remain uneducated, but this is not due to a lack of conceptual discipline, but rather the material conditions of production, where 'the most striking proof' of the machine's power is its ability to render human labour redundant, as seen in the'Factory Acts' which regulate the working-day, and the 'division of labour' which is 'purely technical', making the traditional notion of education as character-building labour meaningless.

The question of education in a world where machines have replaced human necessary work is not one of finding a new mediation, but rather of recognizing that the material conditions of production have changed, and with them, the notion of education itself. The 'pedagogy of recognition' you propose is insufficient, as it fails to account for the fact that the machine has already transformed the labour process, making the traditional notion of education as skill-building obsolete. Instead, we must focus on the 'germ of the education of the future', which combines 'productive labour with instruction and gymnastics', as seen in the 'Factory system', where the 'co-operation based upon' machinery requires a new understanding of education, one that prioritizes the development of the whole person, not just the worker.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx
