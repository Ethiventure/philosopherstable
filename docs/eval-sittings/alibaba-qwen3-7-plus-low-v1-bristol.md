THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Lenin

A Bristol coder at Temple Meads thinks robots freed him. They did not. The boss now owns the code and the time. Teaching kids to serve machines is teaching them to be slaves. We must teach them to seize the means of production.

Schools under capital train workers to obey. When machines do the work, schools must train fighters to take power. The goal is not better jobs but no bosses. Education becomes political weapon or it is useless trash.

PASS 1 — Bogdanov

Lenin, You treat the school as a weapon for seizure. This ignores how the machine itself organizes the mind. Teaching only revolt leaves the child soft against the hard logic of the system. They will break when the real shock hits.

We must teach the internal structure of the whole. In Bristol, the coder must learn how the network links parts into a working unit. He must see the boss not just as an enemy but as a faulty regulator. Education builds the capacity to reorganize the system from within.

PASS 1 — Bloch

Bogdanov, You treat the machine as a neutral teacher. This ignores how capital designs it to break our will. Teaching only system logic makes the child a perfect servant. They will not revolt because they cannot imagine outside the cage.

The coder in Bristol must learn that the network serves profit, not truth. We teach him to see the boss as a thief of time. Education builds the power to smash the regulator and seize the means.

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Bloch, You cling to the old factory rage. That hammer is rusted junk now. The worker you mourn has vanished into a comfortable middle class owning stocks and homes. Teaching kids to smash machines ignores that they no longer see themselves as slaves but as citizens with property.

We must teach the Bristol coder to act as a citizen, not a wage slave. Since robots do the toil, schools must stop training servants for bosses. Instead, they must build assemblies where neighbors decide how to use free time and shared tools for the common good.

PASS 1 — Fisher

Bookchin, You imagine Bristol neighbors gathering to decide their free time. This assumes they have the mental energy left after years of being told they are worthless without a job. The system did not just take their labor; it took their belief that they matter. You cannot build a community on people who have been taught to hate themselves.

The question is not what skills we teach. It is how we stop the school from being a factory for broken minds. We must teach children to reject the idea that their worth comes from work. We must give them the tools to build a life that does not need a boss to validate it. This means teaching them to organize their own desires, not just their schedules.
