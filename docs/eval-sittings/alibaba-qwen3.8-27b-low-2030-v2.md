﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
It’s 2030 and intelligent machines can now do most necessary work cheaper than people — but some say humans need jobs to have purpose. Is that really a problem? What will humans do, who decides and what are the repercussions for society?

PASS 1 — Hegel

In Berlin, a coder watches a machine write his code faster and cheaper. He feels empty without the job. This feeling is wrong. Work was never just a task. It was how people learned to shape the world and each other. When machines take the task, they do not take the need for shared purpose.

The real issue is who decides what we do next. If we only think of work as earning money, we miss the point. We must build new ways for people to connect and create together. The state must step in to guide this, not just let markets decide who gets left behind.

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

You look at a Berlin coder’s empty desk and call his feeling wrong. You are right that the task is gone, but you miss who owns the machine that took it. In my view, purpose does not float in the air; it lives in the hands of people who control what they make. If the owner keeps the profit while the worker gets nothing, the worker feels dead because he is treated as a spare part, not a human being.

The real fight is over who controls the machines that now do most work. Today, factory owners in places like Shenzhen or Detroit decide who eats and who starves. We must take these factories back. Workers shall vote on how to use the new tools. This means ending the rule where one boss decides for millions. The goal is simple: people must own the things they build together.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx

PASS 1 — Bloch

You fix the factory floor, but you miss the kitchen table. In my view, purpose is not just about who owns the tools; it is about what people do with their free time when work stops being a survival task. You are right that bosses steal value, but you treat the machine as a dead thing. I say the machine holds a dream of better living that we have not yet built.

The real fight is over how humans fill their days when machines handle the heavy lifting. If we only take back the factories, people will still feel empty because they lack a shared goal beyond eating. We must build new ways for neighbors to create together, like community gardens or shared workshops in cities like Manchester. People shall decide these projects by open vote, not by market price. This turns free time from boredom into building a warm home for all.

Sources: [83] The Spirit of Utopia — Ernst Bloch

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Bloch, you rightly see that empty hands lead to empty heads. But you treat the machine as a neutral gift waiting for our love. You miss who holds the remote control. In my view, the machine is a tool shaped by the boss’s pocketbook, not a dream of shared life.

The real fight is over who decides what we do when the factory gates close. If owners keep the machines, they will also decide our hobbies and values. We must take back the local meeting hall. Neighbors in small towns shall vote on how to use this new free time together. This stops the rich from ruling our leisure as well as our labor.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin

PASS 1 — Deleuze

You want the town hall to vote on hobbies. That is a trap. If the rich stop paying wages, they will sell us new ways to feel useful. They will turn our free time into a product we must buy back from them. You see the boss’s hand, but you miss how he reshapes our very feelings to fit his market.

Purpose comes from making things that matter, not from having a job title. In Seoul, people are already growing food in vertical towers and coding open-source tools because it feels good, not because a boss pays them. We must build these shared spaces now. The workers shall take over the old factories and turn them into places where anyone can learn a craft or start a project. This stops the owners from selling us fake purpose. The fight is over who designs the daily rhythm of life, not just who owns the machines.

Sources: [38] A Thousand Plateaus — Gilles Deleuze; [39] Bergsonism — Gilles Deleuze
