﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
It’s 2030 and intelligent machines can now do most necessary work cheaper than people — but some say humans need jobs to have purpose. Is that really a problem? What will humans do, who decides and what are the repercussions for society?

PASS 1 — Kant

A warehouse clerk in 2030 watches a machine do her picking, faster and cheaper, and asks what she is for. Ask the question rightly and the trouble shows itself. Work was never the source of human worth. A person is an end in himself, and this means he must never be used only as a tool for someone else's gain. The clerk who fears the machine fears becoming useless to her employer. But her worth never rested on being useful to him. So the worry about purpose is really a worry about who owned her days. If the machine frees her from being a means, nothing is lost. If it leaves her idle while others own what it produces, everything is lost, and the loss was there before the machine arrived.

Consider what nature has always done with us. We are lazy and comfortable by inclination, yet pushed into effort by rivalry with our neighbours, and out of that friction every human ability has grown. A shepherd-life of ease would have left our talents asleep. Remove necessary toil all at once and you remove the spur, and a person left without any task, even a rich one, decays into boredom and small vices. So the question is how a people can keep the friction that develops them once survival no longer demands it. And who decides? The test is simple: only a law the whole people could have given itself may govern this. A company owner or a minister deciding what the idle millions may do with their time is a guardian treating adults as children, and that is exactly the tutelage my essay on enlightenment attacked. Public use of reason must stay free; no generation may bind the next to idleness or to any fixed plan, for that wrongs humanity itself.

Sources: [10] An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment? — Immanuel Kant

PASS 1 — Hegel

Kant, you rescue the clerk by pulling her worth out of her work entirely — the machine frees her, you say, since she was never meant to be a tool. True, and I keep it. But you split the person from the labour that trained her mind. Work disciplined her; take it raw away and the discipline goes too.

So the problem is deeper than purpose: it is who owns what the machines produce and who decides. In Dhaka a garment cutter replaced by a cutting robot still needs wages, a share, a say — or she becomes the discarded rabble my lectures already feared . The state must guarantee her income and seat her on the council that governs the machines. Here is my tension, unresolved: work both chained her and made her free; we must keep the schooling and drop the chains.

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

Hegel, you say the machine frees the worker and you keep the schooling work gave her. Good — I keep it too. But you treat the lesson as the whole story. In Dhaka the cutting robot does not free the woman it replaces; it cuts her wages first. .

So the fight is this: who owns the machines and who sits where decisions are made. The Dhaka cutter must get full income now and a seat on the works council that runs the robots — the garment bosses will resist, and the workers of Dhaka must force it through their unions and streets. What we mourn in lost work is the discipline; what we hate is the boss. Take the boss, keep the discipline, and the freed hours become shared, chosen labour. The tension I hand on: until the machines belong to all, freedom from work is only freedom to starve.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx

PASS 1 — Bloch

You seize the boss's chair and keep the discipline, Marx — I stand on your shoulders for that. But a seat on the robot-council still chains purpose to the shift. The freed Dhaka cutter needs more than a fair share-out; she needs a future worth waking for.

So push the question higher: who decides what humans become once machines carry the load? In Dhaka the unions must take the council — and then cut the working day to four hours, now, so the freed hours open onto teaching, building, music. Purpose is not a job; it is the not-yet, the daydream given hands. I mourn a life measured only by shifts. The tension I hand on: communal plenty can arrive and souls still sit hungry at an empty table .

Sources: [83] The Spirit of Utopia — Ernst Bloch

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Bloch, I stand on your shoulders and I say it plainly — hope needs hands, and you give it hands. But your four-hour daydream still leaves the cutter alone at her kitchen table in Dhaka, waiting for meaning to find her. Your seam is this: who calls the freed hours together? Nobody yet — and that gap is where the bosses will crawl back in.

Here is the harder truth: machines cheapen work, sure, but downsizing is done by grasping owners, not circuits — the very machines that gut jobs could free people from mindless toil if we owned them together . So the fight is over who decides, and that means an assembly, face to face, in every Dhaka neighbourhood, where residents — cutter, rider, teacher — vote on the machines and the shared hours. Municipal councils must confederate across the city now; the minimum is public say over every automated workplace, the horizon is a city run by its own citizens. I hate the thought of plenty feeding bodies while starving souls — so we shall build the table, not wait at it. And if these decisions stay with boardrooms instead of assemblies, no daydream will save us. The open wound: a citizenry is not born free, it must be made, meeting by meeting.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin

Missing from this pale, dead room: the women doing unpaid care work, disabled and queer folk who've never been allowed purpose through a job anyway, and us in the Global South whose labour and land dug the minerals for these machines. PASS 2 — Kant

Bookchin, I love your Dhaka assembly — the cutter, the rider, the teacher voting together. That is my own demand, made flesh. But watch the fault line: a majority in that room can vote the seamstress off the table tomorrow. A gathering of neighbours is powerful only if every person in it counts as an end, never merely a vote to be outvoted.

So we shall bind the assembly itself: a law each could give themselves, guarding the freed machine's product and the freed hours for all — the woman doing unpaid care work in Mirpur included, whom your works councils forget. Now, this year: a public register of every automated workplace in Dhaka, and a binding vote for each displaced worker, paid or unpaid, on what the machine's gain funds. That is the minimum. The horizon is a city whose rules no owner, and no majority, may cancel.

PASS 2 — Hegel

Kant, you are right that a law everyone gives themselves stops the Mirpur assembly voting the seamstress out — the rule guards each person as somebody who counts. But a written rule alone leaves her hungry. Respect on paper does not replace the standing a person earned through work others needed. That is where you stop and I begin.

So the question goes deeper, and I say it plainly: machines in Dhaka now strip work away, and work was how people learned discipline and earned respect from others. Purpose does not fall from the sky; it is built by being counted. Therefore the state — the whole community acting through its laws — must give every displaced worker in this city, and the unpaid women caring for families whom Kant and Bookchin both half-see, a binding seat deciding what the machine's gain funds, this year, by public register. And here the wound stays open: once the machine does the labour, the freed hours have no shape yet. We shall have to decide, together, what a life is for — and no philosopher, dead or living, has finished that argument.

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 2 — Marx

Hegel, you say the community must grant each displaced worker a seat because being counted builds purpose — and there I stand with you. But look at Mirpur again. A seat deciding spending still leaves the seamstress defined by a job the machine ate. Purpose through work was always the boss's bargain; when work vanishes, your respect-by-being-counted goes with it.

The problem is who owns the machines that ate the jobs. In Dhaka, if the robot's product pays shareholders, freed hours mean starvation, and no register fixes that. So we shall take the machines into common hands — now, through the garment-worker councils — so the gain funds half-day work at full pay. The women doing unpaid care, the disabled folk no job ever counted: their seat comes with the community's, not the workplace's. That is the demand.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx

PASS 2 — Bloch

Marx, you stand on my strongest ground and I on your shoulders: the Mirpur garment-worker councils seizing the machines, half-day at full pay — yes, I love that fight, and fear only its smallness. But you leave the seamstress counting purpose in paid hours still. Behind her wait the care workers and disabled women no job ever counted at all. The machine is one link in the chain; the table of labour must become a table beyond labour.

So the question moves higher: who signs off on what a freed life is for, and can Dhaka vote them out? Common ownership is the minimum, this year, through the councils. The horizon: neighbourhood assemblies with real budgets in Mirpur, where purpose is built outside the workplace — music, care, the warmth of a house made human. The workers must demand both, now. What remains open, and we cannot yet name it: what human beings become when labour no longer holds them — that darkness is where we walk.

Sources: [83] The Spirit of Utopia — Ernst Bloch

PASS 2 — Bookchin

Bloch, I have walked on your hope my whole life — that is yours and I honour it. But your freed hours in Mirpur float free of any body that can argue them out. The seamstress with paid hours, the carer with none: both need a seat where their block votes on its own water, wages, machines. Purpose without a decision-room is just leisure somebody else schedules.

So here is my level: the problem is who governs the machines' plenty, and the answer is the neighbourhood assembly itself — face-to-face, every household, one vote, in Dhaka's wards this year. Those assemblies must hold real municipal budgets and send mandated, recallable delegates upward. I fear a freedom that stays private; a purpose nobody votes on rots into TV and pills. The councils seize the shift; the assembly must seize the city. What stays open: whether citizens grown in a market can learn, in one generation, to govern their own lives without bosses.

Sources: [57] Urbanization Without Cities: Beyond Municipalism and Anarchism — Murray Bookchin; [51] The Ecology of Freedom: The Emergence and Dissolution of Hierarchy — Murray Bookchin

Kant and Hegel are arguing about what happens when machines do the work and one man owns the machines. Say a warehouse in Rotterdam goes fully automatic. The loaders lose their pay; the owner gains everything. Both men say this cannot simply be endured.

Kant's point: a machine removing hard labour removes nothing we truly need. What we need is work we choose ourselves. But free choice dies if one owner keeps all the machine's output. So everyone affected must decide together, by law, how the freed hours get used.

On my account, both rest on one thing: people joined together have more power than apart, and a state serves its people only when it increases each person's capacity to act. An owner who keeps the whole harvest keeps the many weak, and the weak fall 'prey to the emotions' — fear, envy, anger — which is exactly what ruins states.
