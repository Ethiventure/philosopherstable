﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
It’s 2030 and intelligent machines can now do most necessary work cheaper than people — but some say humans need jobs to have purpose. Is that really a problem? What will humans do, who decides and what are the repercussions for society?

PASS 1 — Hegel

In the year 2030, a coder in Berlin loses her contract. Her company hands the work to a machine that never sleeps. She is free, everyone says. Free to starve. The shops where she once bought bread now sell nothing a job can buy. This is the old story of the weaving mills, only faster and quieter. When tools become machines, the human being steps aside. The work no longer needs her hands, and soon not her mind. A crowd of people with no work and no property gathers at the edge of the market. They have needs and no means to meet them. That is what the crowd becomes — people left outside the shared life of buying, making and trading, with nothing to offer and nothing to receive. Yes, she is free — free of everything but need. This hungry crowd is not an accident of bad policy. It grows out of the market itself, from the very success of the market. The market pushes work into fewer and fewer hands, and those left out have no way back in. Machines replacing people is only the newest form of this old split .

So the answer to the question 'is purpose really a problem?' is this. Purpose is not something added on top of survival. The two grow from the same root. The human being only becomes a thinking, choosing, planning creature by working on the world and being shaped by that work. As work grows simpler and more cut into pieces, the worker stops thinking. The machine takes over the whole job, and the human being can walk away from it . When that happens, the ground of purpose falls away too. The person is left with time and no place. But here is the twist: the same bare, simplified work that empties the mind also strips away the old brute nature, teaches discipline, and forms habits and skills that make a person master of themselves . Work educates even as it empties. So when machines replace work, we lose both the burden and the training. What will humans do? They will do what people always do when an old world dies: they will grope, quarrel, and slowly build new forms of shared life. Who decides? Not the owners of the machines alone, and not a distant committee. Whoever holds the means of life decides. If the crowd owns nothing and the owners own everything, the owners decide. If the crowd is to decide its own fate, it needs a share in the wealth it did not create but cannot live without.

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

You say the market itself grows the crowd it leaves outside, and you are right that machines are only the newest form of that old split. But your coder still keeps one thing the machine cannot take: the work itself becomes something done to her, not by her. In Capital I showed that one kind of work builds equal value from unequal bodies only because a society already treats men as unequal . The same blindness sits in your machine: it measures work, never purpose.

The Istanbul shipyard welder, renting tools by the day for a courier app, keeps no say over the route or the rate. The question is not what humans will do without jobs. The question is who owns the machines that made the jobs unnecessary. Read the question through the workshop, not the office. The owner of the wharf decides who eats; the crowd that built the wharf must take it. A works council on the Bosphorus shall set the hours and the shares, not the shareholder in Frankfurt.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx

PASS 1 — Bookchin

You set the welder's tools against the wharf-owner who sells them, and you keep your half of the fight: the split stays a split of owners against workers, and machines, you say, count hours and never purposes. Fair. But you stop at who pockets the dock, and the Istanbul machinist on Kartal's shore who tends the arms that make the hulls still takes orders from no wage at all. Owning the wharf is not the same as running the city; the owner you indict is a face of a deeper rank that outlives him.

So the question must climb past wages. If a machine mends more ships than the men on the quays, the quarrel is not who pays them but who commands them. Purpose grows where people decide together what to build, not where they are paid to build it. So let the Istanbul districts gather in their own squares and send men and women, told how to vote and pulled back at will, to a city-wide council for the strait: that council shall set the port's hours, its shares, and its repairs. That is the step we can take now; a full free society of such councils is the far shore we work toward, and whether they can hold the docks against the money-men is the fight still open.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin

PASS 1 — Deleuze

You give the Istanbul machinist the vote and keep the wage split underneath. But your council sits in a square: who chairs it, who counts the hands, who signs the port's ledger? Bookchin, command itself becomes the job, and a job can be abandoned. A council can capture and be captured, and your fresh start is one more order.

So the question grows once more: whenever machines run the wharf, ask who sets the rule and who can break it. In Istanbul, the Kartal machinist, the Kadıköy courier, the Eminönü fishmonger, the Tuzla shipyard fitter must hold not a seat but the power to change what the quay does, together, without a chief. That is our demand, now. The far shore is a life worth living outside work's clock.

Sources: [39] Bergsonism — Gilles Deleuze

PASS 1 — Fisher

You give the Kadıköy courier a vote and the machine still owns the wharf, so you ask who chairs the meeting. Fine. But the one who breaks the rule needs ground to stand on, and Istanbul hands nobody that ground for free.

So we raise it: a worker at the Tuzla yard must tonight hold the tools and the timetable together, and the conveyor must stop when the meeting says stop — this year, in writing, at the yard gate. That is the least demand. What waits beyond work is a city run for living, not for shifts — and we do not yet know how to build the habit of keeping it.

Sources: [71] Ghosts of My Life: Writings on Depression, Hauntology and Lost Futures — Mark Fisher

PASS 2 — Deleuze

You fold Kasimpasa's dockers into the same seat: a recallable delegate and a boss with a schedule look identical under a machine that eats hours. Clastres knew the trick — small groups ward off the permanent chair, but a port council is that chair born. I split purpose from pay, and now split your council from the warding-off you borrow its clothes from: the chief with no weapon is the star who can't stop a berth.

Machines don't need purpose, they need hands that can shut them off. That's the minimum this year; the horizon — the port running for nobody's freight rate but the crews' — sits there, unreached.

PHILOSOPHERS' SERVICE
— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
Everyone at this table is asking one thing: who gets the power that people make together, and who gets pushed around by it. That is the whole puzzle. Hegel says a machine is good or bad depending on who owns it and whether the wealth gets shared — so the tool matters less than the hand on it. Marx takes the same point further: the people who work should hold the machines themselves, not just be handed jobs. Bookchin pushes it out to the city: let the neighbours meeting on their own street decide the port's hours and shares.

You can see these as three answers to one question. Take a warehouse of delivery riders. The app tells them where to go. Hegel asks who owns the app and whether the riders get a real part of what it earns. Marx says the riders should own and run the work, not be hired by it. Bookchin says the riders and their neighbours should set the rules for the whole district.

On my account, all three are chasing the same thing: the power a crowd has when it acts together. A few bosses hold that power, and the crowd does not. The crowd is stronger, but only if it knows it. My *Ethics* puts it plainly: a crowd can hold '<phish>true ideas'</phish> about its own power, and where those ideas are absent, it 'makes complete confusion' — imagine trees that talk, imagine men built from stones. That is exactly how it feels when a rider thinks the app is a natural force and not a set of choices made by particular people.

So the fight is real. The bosses profit. The workers obey. The neighbours are shut out. That is fear doing the ruling .

Does that make it clearer why all three keep returning to the same word — work — but want different hands on it?

Here is the plain test I would put to all of them. Sit at a kitchen table in Hamburg with five flatmates. They argue about the heating bill and who gets the quiet room. But it must survive the kitchen-table test, not just win the vote.
