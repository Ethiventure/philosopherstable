QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Kant

Automation disrupts the traditional relationship between labor and learning, demanding a recalibration of educational aims away from vocational preparation toward the cultivation of autonomous reason.

When machines perform tasks previously requiring human labor, education must shift focus from training for industry to fostering independent moral and cognitive judgment.

Sources: [8] Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch — Immanuel Kant

PASS 1 — Marx

Your claim that education must cultivate autonomous reason ignores the material reality of automation's impact on schooling—machines now perform necessary work, rendering traditional pedagogy obsolete.

Education must align with the new social relations created by automated production, not with abstract ideals of autonomy divorced from material conditions.

PASS 1 — Bloch

Your claim that schools must adjust to automation assumes the old wage relation still exists. Machines have abolished necessary work, freeing humans from labor's yoke.

What must be taught is not skills for a vanished economy, but the awakening of the Not-Yet-Conscious in every child, the stirring of hope beyond survival.

Sources: [85] A Philosophy of the Future — Ernst Bloch

PASS 1 — Weil

You insist that machines have abolished necessary work, but the machine is merely a new form of the apparatus, binding humans to a deeper servitude. The worker is not freed—it is the machine that now performs the task, and the human is reduced to watching.

Schools must teach students how to bear the burden of attention—not to escape it—but to remain awake in a world where even effort has been mechanized.

Sources: [80] Oppression and Liberty — Simone Weil

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Your claim that automation binds humans to deeper servitude is a reductionist slogan that ignores the potential for liberation through democratized technics. Machines are not just tools of control—they are materials for reinventing human labor and learning.

If necessary work is gone, schools must cultivate the ability to govern collectively—a skill honed in face-to-face assemblies, not in solitary reflection or mechanical repetition.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin
