﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Hegel

In Berlin the locksmith's daughter Ilsa watches her father's lathe run itself. She must leave the family's 'feeling of grounded-ness' to become actually, not merely potentially, free.

When machines satisfy needs, education can no longer borrow its seriousness from necessary work; the child's own 'dissatisfaction that draws children to grow up' must be kindled by formation for freedom itself.

PASS 1 — Marx

Hegel, old teacher — I set your dialectic on its feet, yet you still sprout freedom from the child's inner longing. I hate that idealism still breathing! From the machines and their owners comes the school's content, not from spirit. Ilsa in Johannesburg studies in a room shaped by whoever commands the automata.

So we shall teach Johannesburg's children 'productive labour with instruction' — work that makes real things joined to learning — and we shall expropriate, taking into common ownership, the mine-owners and councils who now dictate what a classroom may say, the Commune's lesson applied.

PASS 1 — Bloch

Marx, comrade — you melt down the school into a mirror of the automata; that is mechanical environmentalism, the view that circumstances alone stamp the child, and it betrays your own Thesis 3: the educator must be educated, subjects change circumstances. You teach Ilsa to make; you dread her learning to hope.

So we shall, in Johannesburg, expropriate the classroom too: teach 'natural law' as fought-for dignity, the 'utopistic and the Utopian' distinguished — daydream versus concrete hope, wish versus worked possibility. With toil under 'delegation to machines' , children study freedom's blueprint; the school becomes a workshop of the not-yet-made world.

PASS 1 — Weil

Bloch, friend, you dread the cold fact I wrote in Marseille: no machine abolishes the 'unforeseen' . Your blueprint-school smuggles back the future-worship I tore up — hope as another opiate. I mourn how you dress the void in blueprints.

So teach Ilsa in Johannesburg what automation cannot register : attention — the soul's bare, loving wait on what resists it. A child who sits silent before a broken gearbox, studying it without hurry, learns this better than from any plan.

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Weil, Simone — via Dwight Macdonald's Politics I first took your affliction-and-attention line, and it matters to me still; but you hand Ilsa a mystic's silence, a private gaze before a gearbox, and that waiting reforms nothing. Contemplation without institutions is prayer.

We must teach citizenship. Johannesburg schools become municipal assemblies — tiny practicum councils where Ilsa and her mates decide real matters, water for Alexandra, playgrounds in Hillbrow — rehearsing the 'rebirth of the citoyen' . Robots freed the hours; assemblies must fill them with judgment.


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (after pass 1)
Bro, five dead Europeans, one bougie salon, and not one wage worker, domestic worker, or Global South kid in the diagnosis. Hegel wants schools to manufacture inner dissatisfaction so growth keeps going — that is the most cringe, LinkedIn-mindset take imaginable, honestly, touch grass. The contradiction pass 2 must press: Marx says what gets taught follows whoever owns the machines, while Bookchin says the school itself should run like an assembly — so who owns and runs the automated school, the expropriators or the neighbourhood meeting? Pick one.

These turns floated hard, so drag them down: argue through a sixteen-year-old in a company town where the factory just fully automated and the only owner left is the one who shuttered it — what does Monday look like for her? The visitor question hiding under Bloch's fanciest move is just: how do you teach kids to tell a real shot at a better world from a vibe? Bank it, then: Marx, first step is workers and parents seizing the curriculum committee, not waiting for elections; Bookchin, first step is one school term run entirely by a students-and-staff assembly with real budget power; Weil, first step is kids doing one necessary task badly and daily, feeling the world push back; Bloch, first step is a class exercise sorting actual openings from pipedreams.

PASS 2 — Hegel

Bookchin, you are right that freed hours must be filled with judgment, which is exactly why your assembly-school cannot stand on joy alone: it was need, the pinch of necessity, that tore the child out of the family's sweetness and made him go forth into the world ; your Ilsa in Hillbrow, spared every pinch, may sit in your municipal assembly and deliberate on water while remaining inwardly a child — dissatisfied with nothing, therefore grown into nothing. Your contemplation of the good city reforms nothing if nothing in her suffers.

What now has to be mediated is the school's own task: since necessity no longer drags the child out, the curriculum itself must carry the negation — must generate, deliberately, that dissatisfaction which was once the bitter gift of labour.

PASS 2 — Marx

Hegel, you are right that the child must meet the world's hard resistance — which is exactly why a curriculum manufacturing dissatisfaction in her head fails. Let Ilsa run Johannesburg's automated water-works one term; botch a real repair, feel the pipes refuse .

You hand me back my own contradiction: even expropriated machines need organised producers — so who commands the school-workshop, the ex-workers' council or the neighbourhood assembly?

PASS 2 — Bloch

Marx, you are right that Ilsa must feel the pipes of Johannesburg refuse — which is exactly why handing the school-workshop to a council alone fails: a command from above, even an ex-workers' one, freezes what she touches into finished fact. Praxis, practical world-changing activity, demands the children themselves hold the wrench and the vote — the assembly, not the committee, must break open the curriculum.

So the tension you left burning stands mine too: how does a school run as assembly still teach the discipline the water-works exact? I mourn how easily freedom forgets resistance.

PASS 2 — Weil

Bloch, you free Ilsa to hold both wrench and vote, and truly so; yet your assembly still dreams it can schedule the curriculum in advance, and no vote conjures 'the share of the unforeseen' that the world alone supplies. The children of Johannesburg need the pipe that refuses, not a meeting that plans.

I mourn this: an assembly which votes away all resistance teaches gravity, the soulless pull toward comfort, in the very act of liberating; hand the contradiction on, heavy.

PASS 2 — Bookchin

Weil, Simone — plainly, the discipline of attention is what I carried from your pages into social ecology, my view that social ills and ecological ruin share one root, the way a blocked drain in Soweto and a poisoned river trace back to who commands whom. And you are right that no agenda sheet conjures what the world alone supplies. Which is exactly why leaving Ilsa to comfort's gravity fails: the pipe's refusal must land in an assembly — a room of neighbours deciding together, not a lone soul suffering nobly — a Johannesburg term where the 'rebirth of the citoyen' means a sixteen-year-old answering the burst main with a mandate from her street, 'patient practice' , not passive endurance. Comfort corrodes her; solitary affliction merely sculpts her; citizenship readies her to act.

What your own premise exposes: if the unforeseen educates, then the decisive question becomes who commands the room where it is answered — the owner who shuttered the plant, or the neighbourhood itself; on that my framework still owes a fight.

Bloch, praxis I grant you — yet count-then-claim stays paper while nobody commands the machines the count inventories. Refused.

So, margins, week one has its walker: Ilsa's class elects its recallable Johannesburg assembly, takes the idle robots as common property, and runs them — 'productive labour with instruction' . PASS 3 — Hegel

Marx, You seize the machines, comrade, and I fear you stop there: ownership writes lessons, yet a held tool does not form a soul — what you mourn in the idle factory is real, and still not enough.

Let Ilsa's Johannesburg assembly answer the margins directly: it assigns the difficulty, elected recallably, so no child suffers by a teacher's whim. There the child must go forth from the 'feeling of grounded-ness' into 'dissatisfaction' — the ache that pulls her upward. Slogan: the assembly assigns the ache. Yours seizes tools; mine forms free persons — property is a moment, a passing stage, not the end.

PASS 3 — Bookchin

Hegel, I fear your 'deliberately imposed difficulty' smuggles the schoolmaster back through the assembly door; an ache assigned by mandate reproduces hierarchy, systematic rule of elder over young, wearing a democratic mask — the teacher's whim now wears forty faces.

From you I take the going-forth, the dialectical climb where a thing grows past its given form, and I break from you here: I refuse difficulty administered from above. Ilsa's Johannesburg assembly must 'mutually educate each other' ; paideia, the shaping of citizens through shared civic practice, comes from exercising freedom, and that means the twelve-year-old votes on her own curriculum, she must, or the assembly is a stage set.


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (closing summary)
What I would actually use is Weil's point that only what you undergo teaches you truth, so the first step is kids doing real, recallably-directed unpaid work in their own neighbourhood, not sitting through drills about machines they will never command. And I would take Bookchin's practical one: put children into the local school assembly as full voting members in their first year, starting with one real vote at the next meeting, so self-rule is practiced rather than lectured about.

The genuinely new move was Bookchin's, and honestly it is the most based thing said all sitting: no one assigns the ache, the assembly lives it, and children become full citizens immediately rather than after some cringe apprenticeship in obedience. That is a canon-worthy answer. The weakest move was Hegel's, who managed to say that seizing the machines is not enough until an assembly deliberately re-imposes the very suffering the machines abolished, which is just pain with extra steps, bro, genuinely hard to tell apart from an unpaid internship.
