PASS 1 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

First, picture a person who has trained for years to do skilled work. A machine now does that same work faster and cheaper. The person stands aside, and the machine runs on. We call this progress, but look closer: the machine is the tool that has grown so large and so complex that the human being no longer guides it. The tool once linked a person's will to nature. The machine breaks that link. The worker becomes someone who must beg for the chance to be useful, or must be given money without working, which rots self-respect. And here is the trap: giving the unemployed money makes them dependent, but giving them work makes more goods, and more goods drive prices down, which ruins more businesses and throws more people out of work. Every remedy makes the wound deeper. The system of buying and selling, left to itself, cannot solve this. It forever lacks enough wealth to employ everyone, and it forever produces more than people can afford to buy.

The real question is not who works and who eats^. The real question is what work is for. A person does not work merely to fill a belly. Through disciplined effort, a person learns to master their own impulses and becomes someone who can be respected by others. This is what work gives, even when the task is dull and repetitive. If machines take away that discipline, they do not just take away wages. They take away the way people grow into free adults. So the choice is false: either everyone works at machine speed, or idle people receive handouts and lose their dignity. A third path exists, but it is not the market's path. The community must decide, through its shared institutions, what work is worth doing and who may do it. That is not a loss of freedom. It is the only way freedom survives when machines can do everything cheaply.

PASS 1 — Karl Marx

You say the tool has grown so large that the worker begs to be useful. But you miss who gives the worker away^. The machine is not a runaway tool; it is bought and run for profit. You picture a master and a slave in the workshop. I see the master bound by his own ledger, forced to cut wages or the other moneymen crush him first.

So the real split is not tool versus person. It is between those who own the machines and those who sell their time to use them. When the machine replaces the worker, the worker must bid their own price down, piece by piece, like the London tailors clocking each waistcoat at sixpence. The company saves, the worker's share shrinks. The system survives by casting people aside. The demand: workers must own the machines together and set the pace — not beg to be useful. Until then, who decides is the man with the deepest purse, and he never decides to starve himself.

The live edge: we have not yet invented the institution that lets a person choose to be free of labour without being thrown aside.

PHILOSOPHERS' SERVICE
— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
What are they missing in this conversation?
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
These thinkers treat the machine as if it were a new thing. It is not new. What is new is only the arrangement of bodies ] — which is always a matter of who governs whom.

They argue about who should control the switch, the assembly, the right to define. But none asks the deeper question: what is this machine *for*, in itself? Nothing. A machine has no end. It is just a set of causes and effects, like a river or a storm. It increases or diminishes our power to act, depending on how it is joined to us. The good is not in the machine; it is in the *relation*. ]

So when you ask "what are they missing" — they are missing that the question is not about seizing power over machines. Power is not a thing you grab. Power is the capacity to act, and that capacity grows when we understand what we are and what forms of life increase our joy. The assembly, the switch, the seizure — all these are means. The end is not freedom *from* work, nor freedom to command the machine. The end is the joy of understanding itself.

A delivery rider with three apps is not made free by owning the app. She is made free by knowing why she runs, and whether that running serves her striving or another's profit. That knowledge is the only real possession. The rest is just motion.

Does that show you what they have set aside?

— Immanuel Kant was asked:
What are they missing in this conversation?
— Immanuel Kant answered:
You ask what they are missing. I would say this: they treat the machine as a thing to be owned, seized, or governed — but none asks what the machine does to the *person who uses it*. A machine is not merely an object of power. It is a new relation between a human being and their own action.

When a worker operates a loom or a computer, they are not just serving an owner. They are also exercising — or losing — their capacity to think for themselves. If the machine dictates every step, the worker becomes a mere appendage, a 'given' ] rather than a giver of law. Their will is not their own; it is 'heteronomy' ] — ruled from outside, not by their own reason. The assembly can redistribute the switch, but if the worker still cannot judge what the machine is for, they remain unfree.

A concrete ] case: a delivery rider follows the app’s route, accepts its timing, and never decides a single step. Even if the riders owned the app, they would still be servants of its design. The deeper question is not *who owns the machines* but *who shapes the rules they impose on us*. Until humans act as their own legislators in daily work, no ownership changes that. Do you see how freedom is not just about possession, but about who sets the law of your own action?

They are missing the *organisation of the parts as a whole*. I wrote that any system holds together because its parts do not merely sit side by side — they ‘support each other in definite equilibria’ ]. Two magnets, north and south, are not two things that happen to be near. Each one pulls what the other pushes away; they only exist as poles while that loop runs.
