﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
AGI can now do most paid work cheaper than people, and some say humans need jobs to have purpose. So who works, who eats, who decides — and does the system that paid for the machines survive them?

PASS 1 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

My lectures saw machines throw weavers out of work and make a rabble; handouts deepened the rot [32].

Purpose is not a wage: work shapes us and ties each need to all others [32].

PASS 1 — Karl Marx

You hold that the mind grows through toil, so a wage is beside the point. Yet whoever sets the task owns that growth. Berlin pickers, arms replaced, stay makers still — their hands built the thing that sacks them.

So who works, who eats, who decides. The owner buys the arm, then decides who eats. Warehouse crews shall take the arms and the store, and set the day's work themselves. The system that paid for the machines cannot answer its own question; that gap is the fight.

PASS 1 — Ernst Bloch

You put it strongest: the picker's hands shape the arm that discards them. Yet the shaping leaves a leftover — the daydream of the finished whole. What we build must not swallow us; a made object is worth only as a path back to living people [83].

The quarrel over who holds the arm settles who obeys today, not what the freed hours are for. So the crews must claim the arms and the emptied afternoon together. A Berlin coder will get Saturday for work nobody pays for. That unpaid hour is where the not-yet grows.

PASS 1 — Murray Bookchin

You make the tool a trap and hope the leftover. Bloch, that cuts deep. Yet a Saturday off is still a boss's gift. Hope kept inside paid work changes nothing. The machine stays his; the town stays quiet.

Who works, eats, decides? The town must take the machines and the council hall. Neighbors in Berlin will meet face to face and run the ovens, buses, clinics. One district assembly, then a league, must decide the surplus without a new capital.

PASS 1 — Gillian Rose

You hand the town the machines and keep the meeting. Fair. But who chairs the meeting is already a split: the one who speaks and the one who eats are not yet the same person. A council is not the end of the boss's order. It is the boss's order wearing a new coat, unless the people hold the law and the tools at once.

The split is not owners against workers. It is law against love, and every attempt to flee law lands back inside it. The town that seizes the ovens must also write the rules for who eats. That is its own hard work, not a happy ending. Every district must keep two ledgers: who decides, and who goes hungry. Whoever signs off must also stand in the line.


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (after pass 1)
Marx basically said the point isn't to keep explaining the world but to actually change it. But excuse me, Hegel, 'breaking the wage form' when it's 'not yet real'? That's like saying we'll fix hunger by redefining lunch. Where's the meal? Meanwhile, the actual contradiction: Marx says take the machines, Rose says without rewriting who eats you just rename the boss. Which is it — seize or rewire first? Pass 2 better square that.

Would a visitor ask: 'So if the machines do everything, who gets the free time and who gets the scraps?' Concrete first steps: 1) Crews occupy the machine-owners' arms and run the day themselves (Marx). 2) The town assembly takes ownership of the machines (Bookchin). 3) Whoever wins those arms must also claim the emptied day (Bloch). 4) Rewrite the rules of who eats before seizing, or you just get a new boss (Rose). Missing perspectives: disabled and queer thinkers who know that 'purpose' through work is a trap, and Global South workers whose land and labour built the machines.

PASS 2 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

One hand on the meeting, one on the meal: that is your demand, Rose, and it holds. But paper cannot feed a hand. Writing the eating rule and taking the tools must be one act, or your own split survives inside your fix.

When metal does the doing [32], the human claim is the struggle, not the goods. So crews shall take the machines and write the eating rule in the same hour, every signer standing in the line. The margins note asked where the meal is; the jobless and disabled hands will write it, and the emptied day is theirs.

PASS 2 — Karl Marx

You wrap the whole eating question in one act, and that is strong: no rule without the tools, no tools without the meal. But a written rule is still a thought. You hand the jobless and disabled the empty day. Good. Then tell me who owns the pumped water and the spinning arms. The same taking has to reach the pipes and the looms, or your rule dies at the desk. I have said before: owning the machine, not doing the work, decides who eats. That clause still stands, but now it demands the deed, not the signature.

The one who owns the machine sets the day. Today a warehouse loader in Leipzig watches a forklift lift what lifted his father's back. Cheap robots only get bought where the wage is high enough to beat. So the crew must take the arms and the pipes, and write the eating rule in the same hour. Otherwise the boss keeps the switch. The meal goes to whoever holds the switch.

PASS 2 — Ernst Bloch

You are right, Marx: whoever holds the switch that lifts decides who eats. Yet you let the taking stand as the whole dawn. Seize the arms and you still inherit an empty afternoon. A deed that swaps keepers has not yet learned to breathe.

So the Leipzig loader must take the arms and, in the same hour, claim the free time those arms buy, the seventh day I named in my Spirit of Utopia [83]. The margin note from disabled and queer voices presses hardest here: a job never proved a life. Purpose will be voted, not hired.

PASS 2 — Murray Bookchin

You say taking the switch without claiming the emptied afternoon leaves a hollow dawn. True. But Bloch, hope has no hands. Your free day lasts until the old owner walks back in. The crowd goes home; the plant stays his.

The Leipzig crews must carry the machines into the ward assembly — the open neighbourhood meeting — itself, mandated and recallable, and let it decide what gets made and who eats. That is where the note about disabled people and unpaid carers lands: purpose is voted, never hired. Miss that room and the machines simply change keepers.

PASS 2 — Gillian Rose

The note about disabled people and unpaid carers, you fold into a hall where a show of hands decides who eats. That vote wakes with the same old ache. The room you trust is the market with chairs, and it buys the machines just as the owner did. I hold my old line and shift it: the rules of who eats cannot be voted into being by a room that already ate. That is your genuine fault line: you think the emptied afternoon is free. It is rented, and the landlord is patience. Purpose voted is purpose still licensed, and the license belongs to whoever counts the chairs.

The question of who works is the question of who is allowed to have nothing asked of them. I will hold work and rest in one breath, because they break each other. The machines will be owned by no one, and the food will be claimed by need, not by wage. Every town shall gather its own names, its own disabled, its own tired, and its own bad days, and no assembly shall decide who is useful first. What remains is the ache of a Tuesday when the machines hum and no one knows what to be. That ache is the only honest wage, and it is not yet paid.


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (before pass 3)
Marx said stop just explaining the map; change the terrain. Fair. But Hegel's cute bit about every signer standing in the food line ignores the body that can't stand — that absence stings most. Rose's unclaimed day is burnout with footnotes.

1. Who takes the machine-arms first? First step: a workplace-and-neighbourhood crew lists who controls machines, power, food, then occupies the machine-arms and posts a rota. 2. Who writes who eats? First step: same assembly drafts a food guarantee and a work rota; every signer queues first. 3. What claims the emptied day? First step: assembly taxes machine owners to fund free repair, care, play, study; users set hours.

PASS 3 — Murray Bookchin

Notes from the margins asks who stands in the food line for the body that cannot stand. Rose, your rented afternoon holds while the room stays the market with chairs. Carers sign first.

Bloch says whoever takes the machine must also claim the empty day. Make it a signature: each block writes a food roster on the door, carers and disabled neighbours sign first, the town funds care before counting useful hands. Fight at the warehouse gate. Nothing about us at a meeting we cannot reach. The roster stays empty until the machine sits in the block's hands.

PASS 3 — Ernst Bloch

Your signed door is right, and carers signing first is right. But paper on a door in a block whose machines answer to the warehouse owner is a wish, not a lever: the switch sits outside, and the meal waits on another hand.

So who stands in that line for the body that cannot stand? The block, and the list must hold before the machine turns. Rose keeps half: free hands mean nothing if the day stays empty; inside that open gap grows work people choose, the whole product theirs [83]. Slogan to carry to the gate: nothing turns until the roster is signed.

PASS 3 — Karl Marx

Your signed list is strongest where the body that cannot stand eats first. But paper on a fridge topples when the machine-owner holds the switch [13]. Bloch, your sign begs.

Notes from the margins asks who feeds that body. Every neighbour unable to work shall eat first, by block assembly. Same hands shall seize warehouse, server room and kitchen, then sign the roster before any switch turns. Slogan: no roster, no power. Rose warns a new boss with a new badge remains a boss; carers write the list. Hegel's one act: take machines, feed all. Who guards the guards?

PASS 3 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

You set matter before mind — there is truth in that. Your crew that seizes the switch is still a crew of the able. The machine is a link between hand and world [32]; whoever cannot lift a hand never signs it.

So the margin's question, who feeds the body that cannot stand, becomes the founding rule. Bookchin's town meeting must seat carers and the bedbound signing first. Let each workplace write food list and shift rota in one meeting; let it hold only with their mark. Slogan: nothing about us without our signature. Who holds the pen when the hand shakes?

PASS 3 — Gillian Rose

You make the signature the first law, and truly, a rota written by the able hides the body that cannot stand. Yet the pen is law. Whoever writes who eats keeps the power to name hunger. Your meeting signs a form, and the form signs them.

Let every food list and shift rota be read aloud by the person who cannot work, a carer holding the pen beside them, and posted where the bedridden neighbour can reach it. The fight sits at the care home door: who keeps the record. Does the system that paid for the machines survive them?

PHILOSOPHERS' SERVICE
— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
what are they talking about? make it make sense
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
Your three speakers are circling one question: who gets to command, and who must obey? In my account, every person is a small piece of one whole reality. A small piece is a 'partial negation' [1]. That means each of us has limited power to do things, and we increase it by joining with others. Hegel wants shared laws so work is not a boss's private whim. Marx points to who owns the machine: the owner commands, the worker obeys. Bloch adds that winning the machine is not enough; the worker must also win back the empty day. This is not about free will. It is about feelings that push us: fear, hope, and 'joy' [1], which is pleasure when a past hope turns out well. Take a modern warehouse: pickers wear scanners, a manager sets the pace. If pickers help set the pace, their ability to do things grows. Does that distinction between command and shared power make sense to you?
— Sources shown: Ethics, Demonstrated in Geometrical Order; Treatise on the Improvement of the Understanding (Tractatus de Intellectus Emendatione)

— Immanuel Kant was asked:
what are they talking about? make it make sense
— Immanuel Kant answered:
They are arguing about who commands and who obeys: who owns the machine, who sets the day, who writes the rules for eating. Hegel wants shared laws; Marx wants the crews holding the arms.

What I add is a limit. We only ever see things as they appear to us. What lies behind the appearance, we cannot see. So when a Berlin tenants' meeting is told that owning the block will make them free, I can watch the meeting, the keys, the rent letters. I cannot watch the freedom. That is putting something merely thought where something seen belongs — the old books name it 'a transcendental amphiboly' [5], and it is a very unsafe use of our ideas.

And a rule holds only when the thing it rules over first exists. A judgement can be necessary as a judgement, yet 'the unconditioned necessity of a judgement does not form the absolute necessity of a thing' [5]. First the thing, then its freedom.

Does that land?
— Sources shown: Critique of Pure Reason; Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch

— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin was asked:
what are they talking about? make it make sense
— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin answered:
They are arguing about one plain thing: who holds the lever and who takes orders. Your seats ask whether new laws, or new owners, or a free Saturday settles that. Rose adds the sharp point: taking the machines while leaving the old rules just seats a new boss in the old chair.

Here is my correction. The argument stays stuck in general words. In my book I wrote, 'Calls for action, not in the general, but in the concrete, sense of the term can be made only at the place of action' [42]. Agreeing that workers should own the factory decides nothing by itself. A small group must organise — a paper, paid agitators, local branches — and turn parliament from a 'talking shop' into a working body [42].

Picture riders who take over their delivery app. Without a weekly paper and one paid organiser, the old dispatch manager quietly keeps ruling them from his desk.

Can you see the gap between agreeing who should rule and being organised to make them rule?
— Sources shown: What Is to Be Done?; Imperialism: The Highest Stage of Capitalism; The State and Revolution

— Alexander Bogdanov was asked:
what are they talking about? make it make sense
— Alexander Bogdanov answered:
They are all asking one thing: who gives the orders, and who must obey? Hegel says shared rules. Marx says whoever owns the machine. Bookchin says the town meeting. Rose says new owners with old rules just rename the boss. Every one of them is right about a piece of it.

My own line, on my account, is about joining. Work is people linking their efforts into one whole — through agreement, or through force. Tektology studies that linking in every kind of system, animal or factory or word.

The old order can look steady and still be changing. The books call this 'false equilibrium' [69]: the change goes on quietly, and we miss it because our eyes are weak. Stiff frames also matter — 'degression' [69], the hard shell that locks working parts in place and lets soft, flexible parts grow safely inside.

Picture a Berlin delivery rider juggling three apps. The app is the stiff shell. Whoever owns it decides the route. The riders build their own shell — a shared rota — and the shell protects them.

Did that land?
— Sources shown: Tektology (English full text)

— Simone Weil was asked:
what are they talking about? make it make sense
— Simone Weil answered:
They are arguing about who holds the switch and who gets fed. Hegel says shared laws; Marx says the crews take the machines; Bloch says claim the empty day as well. Each of them wants to answer hunger with a plan.

On my account the whole table is circling one deeper thing. Behind every claim to own, to organise, to vote, sits 'the void' [78] — a hole no wage, no roster, no seizure fills. 'All sins are attempts to fill voids' [78], and every party and every factory committee is such an attempt, dressed as economics.

Picture a Berlin delivery rider who signs her block's food list after a shift. Useful, real. It still does not tell her why she wakes tomorrow. Machines can be seized and the day set free; the hunger for a reason stays exactly where it was.

So the argument is not only about power. It is about who dares admit the hole.

Does that split land — the plan and the hole as two different things?
— Sources shown: Gravity and Grace; Oppression and Liberty

— Gilles Deleuze was asked:
what are they talking about? make it make sense
— Gilles Deleuze answered:
They are asking how a group can arrange daily life so no one becomes the new boss. In my account, a society is a pattern of who can move, who must wait, and what wants are allowed to flow. Power is not a badge you own; it is who decides and who obeys.

Shared laws means a common rulebook. Owning the machine means holding the tools. A free Saturday means time not sold for wages. The table splits over whether new rules, new owners, or free time settles that.

Take Berlin delivery riders juggling three apps. If they seize the bikes but the same dispatcher writes the rota, the boss just changes jacket. A tenants' meeting that writes the food list first is a 'sober gesture' [38] — plain, exact, no flourish. That list is 'a place of passage' [38] from private hunger to shared claim.

Does that make the argument land?
— Sources shown: A Thousand Plateaus (full text); Nietzsche and Philosophy (full text)

— Mark Fisher was asked:
what are they talking about? make it make sense
— Mark Fisher answered:
They're arguing about who commands and who obeys — shared laws, owning the machines, free days, food lists. But underneath, they're circling a lost future. Under what I call capitalist realism — the feeling that capitalism is the only system left — we can't picture a different tomorrow. This is 'left melancholy' [71]: a left that keeps losing without knowing it gave up. The sadness I mean refuses to adjust to what people call reality [71], a 'refusal to adjust' [71]. Take a Berlin delivery rider juggling three apps. She signs her block's food roster after a shift. The roster matters. But she still wakes with no sense tomorrow can be otherwise. That is the void behind the rota. Does that make sense?
— Sources shown: Ghosts of My Life: Writings on Depression, Hauntology and Lost Futures
