QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Spinoza

Automation removes labor, but not the necessity of teaching children to act from adequate ideas.

If machines replace human work, the task of education shifts from training function to cultivating clarity of mind and power of acting.

Sources: [3] Tractatus Theologico-Politicus — Baruch Spinoza

PASS 1 — Kant

Your claim that education must cultivate clarity of mind assumes reason is innate, but reason requires habituation to the moral law.

When automation replaces labor, education must instill the habit of autonomously legislating according to universalizable maxims, not just clarify thoughts.

Sources: [8] Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch — Immanuel Kant

PASS 1 — Hegel

You posit that education must cultivate habit of legislation, yet this remains one-sided because it neglects the sedimentation of ethical love in the family.

Where the issue truly unfolds is in the dissolving family, where children learn grounded-ness before entering the system of needs.

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

You claim education must cultivate legislative habits, but this merely conceals the erosion of familial grounding, where children lose the soil of lived ethics before entering the system of wants.

What is needed is not legal training but a reorientation of learning around the dissolution of labor itself, where automation strips away the old tasks and leaves students untethered from purposeful action.

PASS 1 — Lenin

Your claim that automation frees children from labor neglects the ruthless restructuring of social reproduction, where schools become instruments of cognitive regimentation, not ethical formation.

Education must confront the devaluation of human labor by cultivating resistance to algorithmic governance, ensuring students master not tools but the critique of systems that commodify cognition.

Sources: [46] Materialism and Empirio-Criticism — Vladimir Lenin

PASS 1 — Bogdanov

You assume education remains a site of ethical formation, but this neglects the structural shift where schools become sites of cognitive regimentation, not liberation. The problem isn’t mere resistance to algorithms—it’s the failure to reimagine education as a dynamic system of collective cognition.

The core organizational problem lies in the breakdown of pedagogical coherence, where outdated methods clash with the radical reorganization required by automated labor. Education must evolve beyond rote skills into a scientifically organized field of adaptive thinking.

Sources: [58] Tektology: Universal Organization Science (Book 1) — Alexander Bogdanov

PASS 1 — Bloch

Your claim that schools must become sites of 'adaptive thinking' misses the deeper rupture—machines have ended the division of labor, rendering all training obsolete. The worker is extinct, and with him, the last justification for schooling.

What remains is not adaptation but awakening—a rediscovery of the Not-Yet-Conscious in daily life, outside classrooms and curricula.

Sources: [85] A Philosophy of the Future — Ernst Bloch

PASS 1 — Weil

Your claim that schools must become sites of 'adaptive thinking' misses the deeper rupture—machines have ended the division of labor, rendering all training obsolete. The worker is extinct, and with him, the last justification for schooling.

Education must abandon the delusion of preparation for a vanished future and instead confront the raw immediacy of now—an encounter with the body, the earth, and the silent burden of survival.

Sources: [80] Oppression and Liberty — Simone Weil

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Your claim that machines have rendered all training obsolete misunderstands the enduring necessity of education—not as preparation for labor, but as cultivation of the citoyen, the politically conscious citizen who governs through deliberation and solidarity. Training is not dead—it has evolved into the formation of a liberated subject.

What must be taught is not skills for a vanished job market, but the art of self-government, ecological responsibility, and collective inquiry—a curriculum shaped by municipal assemblies and grounded in the lived experience of Mexico City’s neighborhoods.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin

PASS 1 — Deleuze

You fix 'citoyen' as a stable identity, but this traps the issue in a historicist schema that denies the becoming of political subjectivity.

Education must cultivate lines of flight, not citizens—teach kids how to detach from fixed roles and engage with the flows of desire and technics shaping Mexico City.

Sources: [38] A Thousand Plateaus — Gilles Deleuze

PASS 1 — Rose

You claim to have escaped the citizen, but this trap remains essential to the political subject—a paradox that betrays your refusal of dialectic.

Education must confront the broken middle between automation and human purpose, teaching children not detachment but responsibility toward the structures that govern them.

Sources: [95] Your Visit to Auschwitz — Gillian Rose

PASS 1 — Fisher

Your claim that education must teach responsibility to structures is a siren song that masks the collapse of those structures. Responsibility assumes legitimacy, but the system is already hollow.

We must teach children to dismantle the myths of necessity that automate their labor and erase their agency—before they become complicit in their own obsolescence.

Sources: [71] Ghosts of My Life: Writings on Depression, Hauntology and Lost Futures — Mark Fisher
