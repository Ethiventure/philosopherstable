QUESTION
AGI can now do most paid work cheaper than people, and some say humans need jobs to have purpose. So who works, who eats, who decides — and does the system that paid for the machines survive them?

PASS 1 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

The idea that a job gives a person their worth is a trap. I saw this in my time, when machines stripped tens of thousands of weavers in England of their daily tasks and left them with only misery [32]. A person is a mind that shapes the world, not a hand that moves a lever. If the mind drives away from the lever, the person does not vanish, but the old way of paying them vanishes. The factory owner who buys the machine finds his profits shrinking as the simple work disappears. The tool was once the link between a human thought and the outside world. Now the machine runs on its own gears. The worker is left out. The profit system that bought the machine now has no one to buy its output. The owner is stuck with a machine he cannot sell to, and the worker is stuck with a life that has no value in the market. The system that paid in wages is breaking under the weight of the thing it made.

The problem is not where the work goes. The problem is how a person who is no longer needed for making goods still finds a place in the shared life of the town. We think in terms of wages, but a person’s life has more in it than money. The question becomes how the whole community, the family, the city council, and the civic groups, decide together who gets to eat and who gets to speak. It is about moving away from the idea that your worth is set by a boss. It is about finding a new way for people to see themselves as full parts of the social whole, not just as bodies that trade time for food.

PASS 1 — Karl Marx

You treat the worker as a ghostly mind trapped in a body. That is nonsense. In the London mills I watched, the machine does not just take the task; it changes the worker’s muscles and eyes into parts of the gear system. The human does not vanish; they become a slower, cheaper replacement for the piston. The profit system does not break because it has no buyers. It breaks because it burns through the living human body while counting only the hours the body moves. You think the job is a label; I see the wage as a chain.

The machine eats the worker’s time until the worker has none left to buy the machine’s goods. This is the real crisis. If the robots make the bread, the factory owner still needs someone to eat it. The workers must seize the factories, not just ask for a handout. In a Berlin warehouse today, a driver must know that the app on their phone is a whip. They must stop driving when the whip cracks. The community must decide who eats, not the owner. Freedom means owning the tools, not begging for a task.

PASS 1 — Ernst Bloch

The pile of free goods is a promise, not a prison. That pile is the physical thing waiting for us to claim it for common use.

The robot does not kill the future; it breaks the shell holding it in. We must seize the factories now, not argue later. Real people in Ruhr warehouses tonight: stop the app, take the keys. The hungry know they are waiting for this free time. Wake up. The dawn is here, but only if they move beyond their jobs into the full life waiting. Who pulls the plug on the owners? That is the question burning now.

PASS 1 — Murray Bookchin

You wait for a golden morning after the grind stops, but hope is useless if the bosses still hold the keys. The machines are real, yet they run in fenced parks owned by a few. Taking the tools is a start, but they will hide the smart parts in the cloud. You break the shell, but you leave the eye in the tower. That eye watches who eats and what gets made.

We must shatter the gates and march on the server farms tonight. The hunger in the Ruhr district proves who starves under this rule. We demand the local town hall take control of the power grid and the delivery trucks. Citizens sit in the square, not the office, and vote on what to build and who to feed. This is how we turn cold tools into living tools. Hope is the act of taking the wheel now.

PASS 1 — Gillian Rose

Bookchin sees the gated park but misses the debt inside it. The worker who builds the server farm is paid to own nothing but their labour. This money is only good because everyone agrees to trade it. The gate doesn’t just lock people out; it turns human care into a dead ticket. To smash the wall is to ignore the debt that built the wall. The profit from the machine comes from unseen hours of pain that were counted and discarded as if they were waste.

We must stop asking who owns the server and start asking who pays for the code. Every click you make creates a ledger entry that separates you from your own need. The local council cannot vote on meaning while the global debt ledgers keep ticking. The worker in the data centre shifts must demand their time counts as real life, not just a cost. We shall force the boardroom to answer for the hours they erased. The machine eats because we taught it to value speed over presence. We must make the profit show its face and take the blame for the hunger it grows.

PHILOSOPHERS' SERVICE
— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
What are they talking about? make it make sense
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
Imagine two workers in a Berlin factory. The first says, "We have too many machines; we need a new law to give us a job." The second says, "The machines did not exhaust us. The owners just don't need our tired bodies to make profit anymore." They are arguing about the same event.

On my account, the first worker sees a gap. This is what I call 'partial negation' [1]. The future looks dark because something is missing. The second worker sees the whole chain. He knows the cause is not a lack, but a limit on the owner’s ability to take value. This understanding brings a strange calm. It is not 'joy' in the happy sense, but the clear idea of 'something past' that explains the present pain [1].

The key is power. When you understand why you are tired, you control less of the feeling. You stop blaming the machine. You see the owner’s need for cheap bodies as the real cause. This is how we start to fight back. We do not fight the steel gears. We fight the system that feeds us into them.

Do you see how knowing the true cause changes how you feel about the pain?

— Immanuel Kant was asked:
What are they talking about? make it make sense
— Immanuel Kant answered:
You face two very different ideas about why we feel lost. Spinoza points to a machine owner who needs less labor. Hegel says the machine changes how society works. Marx argues our bodies are just tools for profit. That sounds like a fight about factories, law, and what makes a human life good.

From the passage above, I say we often get stuck on "verbal definition" [5]. We use a term without checking if we can actually picture the thing behind it. We also assume we know "things in themselves" [5]. We think we understand a system better than we really do. This is a trap. It stops us from seeing how simple names hide complex forces.

Look at a delivery driver in Berlin today. An app tells him to drop one parcel. The driver thinks the delay is his fault. But the app’s code is a "thing in itself." It is a hidden rule. The driver only sees the red screen. The driver treats the screen as the whole truth. He does not ask how the code was built or who benefits from the speed.

Can you point to one rule in your daily life that hides its own source from you?

— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin was asked:
What are they talking about? make it make sense
— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin answered:
You are asking how to understand their debate. Let’s look at a scene. A delivery rider in London checks five phones at once. He is fast, but the company owns the route. If he stops, he starves.

The philosophers above are fighting over why this rider feels trapped. Spinoza says the owner’s greed is the locked cage. Hegel says the whole delivery system changed how we think. Marx says your tired body is just a tool for making money.

On my account, you need to see the specific role of organised workers. I call this 'live and striking exposures' [42]. This means catching the boss doing something illegal or unfair right now, and shouting it out to everyone. You do not need a written letter asking people to act. The action calls itself.

Consider the mistake of thinking the problem is just 'purely economic'. That is an empty phrase. It is as meaningless as saying 'development is proceeding' towards cooking food in labs [42]. It ignores who controls the lab. The rider must organise with other riders to seize control of the routes, not just wait for the system to fix itself.

Do you see how pointing at the specific owner’s greedy act is stronger than talking about general economic trends?
— Sources shown: What Is to Be Done?; Imperialism: The Highest Stage of Capitalism

— Alexander Bogdanov was asked:
What are they talking about? make it make sense
— Alexander Bogdanov answered:
Think of a factory where each machine does one tiny job. The parts fit together to make a car. This is how society works too. Different groups do different jobs. They connect to make a whole society.

Our word for this is “organisation.” It means how parts link up to work as one thing. In biology, cells change and specialize. But if a cell changes too much, it stops working with the rest. So, nature needs a balance. I call this “contra-ifferentiation” [69]. It keeps the whole body alive.

Look at a food delivery app. The driver, the customer, the kitchen, and the server all link up. One person cannot deliver a hot meal alone. The app organises these links. If one link breaks, the meal is cold. The other thinkers argued about who is sad when the link breaks. I look at how the links are built. Is the connection strong? Does it help everyone? Or does it trap some people?

To fix society, we must build better links. We need a plan for how people and machines work together. This is about building a working whole, not just blaming one boss.

Do you see how the delivery app acts like a living system of linked parts?

— Mark Fisher was asked:

The group above fights over why you feel empty. They look at a delivery rider stuck in London traffic. Spinoza blames the boss’s greed. Marx says your body is just a tool for profit. They miss the heart of the matter.

On my account, the problem is a specific feeling. I call it 'hauntological melancholia' [71]. This means being haunted by futures that never happened. We are stuck in a mood where the past feels weightless and the future is flat. It is not just sadness. It is the cancellation of hope.

Look at your phone screen. It shows you endless choices for what to watch or buy. But it offers no vision for how life could be different. The past is recycled endlessly, like old movies in new costumes. You can choose a brand, but you cannot choose a world. This flatness is the real trap. It makes any change seem impossible.

Do you see how the feeling of being stuck is not just personal, but a result of what is shown to you as possible?
