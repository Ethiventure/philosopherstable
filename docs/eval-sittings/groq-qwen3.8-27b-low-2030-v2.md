QUESTION
It’s 2030 and intelligent machines can now do most necessary work cheaper than people — but some say humans need jobs to have purpose. Is that really a problem? What will humans do, who decides and what are the repercussions for society?

PASS 1 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

The idea that humans must work to find purpose assumes that value comes from the act of doing, not from where the person stands in the social whole [32]. When machines took over weaving in England, the workers did not find new meaning in the loom; they found misery because the social arrangement trapped them in a role that no longer had a shape [32]. Purpose is not a private feeling inside a worker’s chest. It is the result of the community recognizing the individual as a free, thinking being who contributes to the shared life. If the machine does the pushing and pulling, the human being is free to look at the work and see it for what it is, without the blindness of routine [32]. The problem is not the idle hand. It is the idle life.

We must ask who arranges the world when the tilling and weaving are done. If the state and social groups do not give people clear, respected places, the empty time becomes a void [32]. The machine is a tool that lets nature do the heavy lifting, so the human mind can rise above the grunt work [32]. This shift needs a new social shape. The state must step in to provide the conditions where people can be fully human, not just cheap parts in a factory gear. If the rich own the machines and the poor are left with nothing to do, society breaks. The new order must link the person’s inner freedom with their outer role in the community. Otherwise, the machinery serves only the few who own it.

PASS 1 — Karl Marx

You grant that a worker’s standing in society shapes their sense of having a place, yet you treat this standing as a feeling granted by the community. That is the mistake. Standing is not a gift from polite circles. It is the cashmere coat spun in a Manchester mill. When the machine stops needing human hands, the social bond you describe dissolves into a void because the worker is cut off from the tools that make life. You speak of an empty life, but empty time under the current rule is just hunger with a polite name.

The group that owns the smart machines decides who eats and who waits in line. In 2030 Berlin, a coder who built the autonomous fleet sits in a warm room, while the former driver sells goods at a street stall to pay rent. The driver has no share in the new way of making goods. The working class must seize the machines and organize production for all. We shall end the rule of the few owners and make the new tools serve every human being.

PASS 1 — Ernst Bloch

You spot the owner’s warm room and the driver’s cold stall. That is correct. But you stop at grabbing the tools. Seizing the machine allows the driver to trade hours for bread. A new boss appears. The owner leaves, but the worker remains a wage slave with a different pay slip. You fix the pay, not the purpose.

The driver must not just pick up a new tool. People need to wake up with something to build. In 2030, city councils decide who gets free time. The workers shall draft a list of urgent fixes. They will repair a leaking roof in the old district. This act gives the driver a role that builds a home, not a profit.

PASS 1 — Murray Bookchin

You see the worker needing a reason to rise, which is right to keep hope alive. But fixing a roof for the council leaves the boss holding the keys. You trade one owner for another. The driver still obeys commands given by a few men in an office, just with nicer bricks.

People must govern the work they do, not just do it. In 2030, street meetings in your own neighborhood will decide who fixes the water and who builds the park. No mayor, no manager, no app decides. Citizens sit together, speak up, and vote on how to use the free time machines create. We must take back control from the few.

PASS 1 — Gillian Rose

Bookchin hands the keys back to the street. This keeps the person trapped in their current role. The laborer remains the worker. The driver stays the driver. If the machine takes the task, the human loses their reason for being. You fix the boss but keep the job. The split remains hidden in the act of working itself.

The machine kills the debt of owed labor. This frees us from having to survive by serving a boss. We must use this free time to rebuild what the job destroyed. Neighbors must sit together not just to vote on water, but to heal the isolation work caused. We will claim this idle time for human connection. The cost is staring into a void that no paycheck can fill.


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (after pass 1)

You keep yapping about seizing the robots, but ignore that the 'rational state' Hegel loves is just a suit-and-tie club where the rich get a place and the rest of us are ghosts? That’s the core contradiction you missed: Hegel says freedom is owning your time in a recognized state, but Marx says you can’t have freedom if you don’t own the means of production. How is 'recognition' from the state enough if the state is still locked down by the owners? Pass 2 needs to figure out if you can actually be free inside the cage.

Missing Perspectives: We need the queer and disabled voices to smash these straight, ableist ideas about what a 'normal' life looks like. PASS 2 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

Rose keeps the person on the shop floor even after the boss is gone. She says we must sit together to heal the pain of work. This saves the heart but holds onto the old cage. The pain came from doing tasks for someone else’s profit. If you just stop the work but keep the mindset of needing a task to matter, you stay stuck. The goal is not just feeling better. It is changing who gets to decide what matters.

The question is who gets a seat at the table when the machines do the heavy lifting. In my view [32], freedom means having a recognized place in the community that decides its own laws. It is not just about having free time. It is about having power over the future. If the rich own the robots, they own the rules. The working class must take control of the institutions that set the laws. Everyone, including those whose bodies do not fit the old mold, must have a real voice. This is the only way to end the fear of being useless. The state must be built by all, not for the few. This changes life from surviving to building together.

PASS 2 — Karl Marx

Hegel gives you a vote but keeps the bosses holding the money. You call for power over the future, which is good. But a seat at the table means nothing if the table is owned by the factory floor. He saves the feeling of belonging but holds onto the cage of private profit. The rich do not need your approval if they own the robots that make the food and shelter.

We must take the machines from the owners. This is not about fixing feelings. It is about who presses the start button. In a Berlin park in 2030, neighbors vote on what to build next. The old law that says a body is useless if it cannot lift heavy things is burned. A disabled neighbor decides the shape of the new path. You vote with your whole life, not just your hours. This must happen now. The workers and the excluded shall break the private lock on production today. Freedom starts when the community holds the tools, not the bosses.

PASS 2 — Ernst Bloch

Marx hits the right note: who owns the robots decides who eats. Yet he looks only back at the owners, not forward at the humans. He treats the worker as a tool in a machine. This misses the human spark inside every person. I build on his fire, but I look to the dawn, not just the wreckage.

In 2030 Berlin, a care worker and a coder share a park. They no longer trade hours for coins. The question shifts from what can I do? to what shall we become? Everyone, including those with different bodies, must vote on local projects. No boss decides. This freedom is real only when every single body feels at home in the public square.

PASS 2 — Murray Bookchin

You say every body must feel at home in the square. But who builds the square? If rich owners set the rules, the disabled and queer still sit on the cold bench. You want hope, but you leave the keys in the landlord’s pocket. That is not freedom. That is a party at somebody else’s house. I owe you that trust in human sparks, but sparks die in a room where the boss picks the guests. Your local vote is only free if the people holding the tools are the same people holding the pencils.

Break the owner’s grip on the tools, not just their voice. In 2030, the city assembly must own the robot factories. A coder in Berlin and a disabled neighbor in Istanbul sit together to map local needs. The robot stops being a tool for cheap labor and becomes a friend helping humans breathe. We must demand the local council control the machines. If you cannot vote on the factory, you do not own your life. The trauma heals only when the cage opens from the inside, with our own hands. Who decides what the machine builds is who decides what humanity is.

PHILOSOPHERS' SERVICE
— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
what are they talking about make it make sense
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
Five philosophers look at one broken picture: a robot makes the stuff, the human gets nothing to do. They ask how a person finds purpose when work is gone. My answer is that purpose is thinking clearly.

Think of your mind like a muscle. It gets stronger when you understand why things happen, not when you just react to them. That understanding is your power. When a machine takes your labor, you do not lose your nature. You lose the fake need to prove your worth to a boss. Your power grows when you stop obeying outside commands and start using your own logic.

On my account, true joy comes when you see the full cause of an event. From the passage above, joy is "pleasure accompanied by the idea of something past," [1] meaning you feel good when you understand a past struggle by seeing its real causes. This is not waiting for an order. It is acting from your own understanding.

Picture a Berlin coder who quits a dead-end tech job. He uses his free time to teach his neighbors how to fix their own networks. He is not working for pay. He is acting from his own true power. He feels "disappointment" [1] only if he stays stuck in confusion. When he builds his own rules, he is free.

Have you ever felt stronger after solving a hard puzzle on your own, rather than being told the answer?

— Immanuel Kant was asked:
what are they talking about make it make sense
— Immanuel Kant answered:
They are arguing about what happens when machines take your job. Is your worth tied to your daily task?

My view is simple. Your value does not come from your salary or your role. It comes from your ability to reason freely. This thinking is your true self. When a robot packs a box, it follows rules. You, as a human, answer to the moral law inside you. You decide right from wrong. That power is real. It is independent of what the economy pays you for.

In the *Critique*, I argue we cannot fully know things as they are "in themselves" [5]. We only know them as they appear. This means your inner moral duty is the only thing you can truly grasp. No robot can outsmart your conscience.

A concrete example: A Berlin coder loses their job to automation. They feel useless. But they now have time to serve in a jury or help a neighbor. They decide what is fair. This active choice is where dignity lives.

Does your sense of self depend on your paycheck, or on your choices?
— Sources shown: Critique of Pure Reason

— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin was asked:

— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin answered:
They are arguing about a world where robots do the physical work. Your job disappears. What comes next?

Hegel says you get a legal spot in a fair society. Marx argues the machines belong to rich owners, so workers must seize control to regain power. Bloch says you find meaning by fixing broken things, not by holding a title. Bookchin says neighbors must meet and decide together what to build. Rose says the time you used to spend for a boss now belongs to you. You must heal the damage work caused, or you lose yourself.

Think of a Berlin coder. He builds apps that track delivery riders. The riders have no time for their children. On my account, if the workers take over the machines, the coder does not trade one owner for another. He uses the new free time to teach his child math, instead of writing code for a CEO. The purpose is building the next generation, not packing boxes.

Do you see how the machine’s owner decides who lives well, and why that owner matters?
