﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
It’s 2030 and intelligent machines can now do most necessary work cheaper than people — but some say humans need jobs to have purpose. Is that really a problem? What will humans do, who decides and what are the repercussions for society?

PASS 1 — Hegel

Those who lament lost purpose fix upon labour as an end; yet labour was always mediation, discipline by which need tears the human being out of himself . The machine that lets the worker 'can walk away from it' sublates toil, cancelling its necessity while preserving the education it contained .

The true danger is that liberated individuals, severed from recognition through work, sink into 'the rabble' ; the state must mediate what civil society abandons. Who decides is the question: the universal will, or the owners of the machines?

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

Hegel, you keep what matters: toil was always mediation, discipline tearing men from mere need. But you speak of the Idea sundering itself, as if machines freed labour by Spirit's deed. Your dialectic stands on its head. What walks away from the worker in Prague's automated mills is capital, 'a machinery system' , and the purpose-question hides a property-question.

I drop your state as healer — it is the owners' committee. I inject this: machines only liberate when the producers own the conditions of production. Until then, cheaper work means cheaper humans, and I fear that rabble, made by expropriation, never by nature. So the workers' councils of Smíchov shall shorten hours — seize the 'diminished length of the working-day' as freedom's first measure — and demand social ownership of the automata now, with communal control as the horizon, not a gift granted.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx; [28] Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right — Karl Marx

PASS 1 — Bloch

But you count the liberated hours as if they were the goal. Shortened hours in Smíchov are the threshold, never the homeland. You break where the Front begins: what does the freed worker walk toward?

I reject your ending at labour's gate. I inject this: purpose is not the job's shadow; it is the Not-Yet-Conscious — the unformed longing that daydreams ahead of every job description. Machines that disburden must stay kept down, merely servants, so the whole human being — not the employed fraction — steps forward . Prague's councils shall therefore fund waking-dream workshops, studios, and communal building — 'the table of labor, beyond labor' — paid, dignified, open to every ex-mill worker from Vinohrady to Karlín. Now: shorten hours; own the automata; then build the warm world the hours buy.

Sources: [83] The Spirit of Utopia — Ernst Bloch

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Bloch, you were right to damn the machine to servanthood — I keep that. But your waking-dream studios are meadows for private souls. I hate to say it of you: longing without a public sphere curdles into lifestyle. Purpose is 'a new citizenry' , a 'body politic' with faces.

I drop the workshop as end-point; I inject citizenship. Prague's district assemblies — Smíchov, Karlín — must decide the automata's product, not merely dream beside them. Tomorrow the mills' ex-workers shall vote policy there; engineers administer only. The horizon: confederated municipalities replacing the state, deliberately begun now.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin

PASS 1 — Deleuze

Bookchin, you loved flagging our desiring-machines — a decade of relish, that. Keep the assemblies: Karlín's mill-votes are a real assemblage, flows given collective hands. But citizenship fixes faces into identities, a molar grid. You confederate municipalities yet preserve the State's very diagram — representation.

I drop the citizen as unit; I inject becoming. Machines in 2030 modulate us continuously — control, not factory discipline. So the assemblies must stay porous, hooked to contagion between districts, between humans and automata, users and tinkerers. Purpose is not conferred by a vote; it is produced as 'creative emotion' crossing Smíchov workshops tonight. The mill-ex-workers must, this year, seize open platforms — patents, code, the automata's schedules — and experiment in public. What I fear: your free communes recaptured, smoothly, by the very feeds that manage them.

PHILOSOPHERS' SERVICE
— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
Three philosophers are arguing about what happens to a worker when a machine takes their job. Hegel says the worker loses more than wages: work is where others confirm your worth, so a replaced worker becomes invisible. Marx says follow the money: whoever owns the machines keeps the wealth, so the workers should own them together and work fewer hours. Bloch says freed hours are a gift: use them to build and try new ways of living.

On my account, they all miss the simplest point. A person's essence is striving — this means every one of us pushes to keep existing and to do more, not from duty but from our nature. A purpose given by a job can be taken away; the power to act cannot, if you keep it. What matters is whether freed hours increase that power or shrink it. A Dutch warehouse worker given a state payout and a shared workshop grows stronger; one given only a screen and pity falls into what my Ethics calls the 'dejected', who are 'a prey to the emotions' .

Ask yourself: does your free hour make you able to do more, or only to watch?

So: who should hold the parts together — the owner, the meeting, or the workers themselves?

— Mark Fisher was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Mark Fisher answered:
They are arguing about purpose. A robot has taken a man's warehouse job, and each thinker gives a different account of what he has lost and what could replace it. Hegel says worth in other people's eyes. Marx says the money and the machines. Bloch says free time to test new ways of living. Bookchin and Deleuze argue over who decides — a town meeting or whoever holds the robot's instructions.

On my account, they all share a belief I want to pull out into the light: that the plan is the hard part. It is not. The hard part is that we have lost the ability to believe any plan could happen. I call that capitalist realism — this means a mood where capitalism feels as unavoidable as weather, so nobody even tries to picture anything else.

Watch how it works. In 2007 people queued for a new phone; in 2008 the crash came, and the same people accepted bailouts for banks and cuts for themselves without a protest worth the name. The dream-rooms Bloch wants, the assemblies Bookchin wants — most people would call them lovely and impossible in the same breath. That double move is the cage.

So I differ from Rose's warning about getting lost in dreams only in this: mourning and melancholia — this means grieving a loss versus refusing to let it go — need reversing. Brown attacks a left 'attached to its impossibility', in love with its own failure . But there is a good stubbornness too: refusing to accept the current setup as reality, even if you feel like an outcast in your own time .

The concrete move: the freed hours must produce new culture that makes another world feel possible again. Does that cage feel familiar to you?
