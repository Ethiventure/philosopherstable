﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
It’s 2030 and intelligent machines can now do most necessary work cheaper than people — but some say humans need jobs to have purpose. Is that really a problem? What will humans do, who decides and what are the repercussions for society?

PASS 1 — Hegel

Watch a Berlin coder whose code the machines now write themselves. He has no work, no wage, no need to plan his day. Those who say he has lost purpose speak half a truth. Work tears a person out of idle comfort and teaches discipline. But purpose was never the machine's job to give or take.

The real tension: my work also fed others, and their work fed me. When machines do that work, the tie between people loosens unless new shared duties are built. Who decides? The community, through its laws — else machine owners rule, and a bitter class of the left-out grows, as it did among English weavers. How people learn to matter to each other without wages — that remains unresolved.

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 1 — Marx

Hegel is right on one thing: the coder without work has lost more than money. Work taught him to matter to others; their work fed him, his fed them. You trace that bond through shared doing, and there you rescue what the wage-glorifiers drop. But you stop at the tie between neighbours. The tie is enforced through wages, and whoever owns the machines now collects from everyone. Purpose was never the real stake. The machine owners decide, and the rest obey.

So ask differently: when a warehouse robot in Ohio pays no wage, whose table does the former picker eat from? Capital takes the whole product and hands back charity, if it hands back anything. The workers and their unions must take the machines into common ownership and shorten the working day for all, not beg for purpose. Aristotle could not see equal human labour behind unequal things because slavery hid it from him ; our age hides the same truth behind the machine. The unresolved edge: people who have never decided anything together must now learn to run production without a boss above them.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx

PASS 1 — Bloch

Marx, you rightly keep work as shared doing — I stand on your Theses there. But you treat purpose as a wage-question only, and the wage dies with the picker. The machine in Ohio leaves a second gap you skip: not just income, but the daydream that pulled people out of bed. Hunger is the start, never the whole road.

So the fight moves past ownership alone. The unions shall seize the robots AND shorten hours so freed time becomes school, workshop, garden — real place for hope. Who decides? Everyone at the table, learning to rule together. The open edge: what do we build with the saved evening?

Sources: [84] The Principle of Hope, Vol 1 (Introduction) — Ernst Bloch; [86] Commentary on the Theses on Feuerbach — Ernst Bloch

PASS 1 — Weil

Bloch, you rescue the freed evening as a place for hope, and there I stand with you. But watch what fills that evening already: screens run by the same machines that took the work. An empty hour is not free; gravity fills it. A warehouse picker given time but no attention, no hard craft to learn against, only a feed scrolling — that is a daydream replaced by a drug, and the boss still profits.

So the problem is deeper than who owns the robots. Work was never mainly pay; it was the one place where thought touched stone, wood, iron, and the soul grew by obeying real things. Take that away and people swell into crowds, easy meat for any party or platform that offers them a purpose. In 2030 that means company towns of app-tracked delivery riders and content shovellers — cogs wearing headphones instead of gloves. Who must act? The workers themselves, in their unions and local councils, shall demand that every necessary machine hour bought be paid into a fund for craft: workshops, gardens, trades taught by hand, run at street level, never by a ministry. The open wound stays: a machine can copy our doing, never our consent to necessity — and nothing in this settlement guarantees anyone will still want to consent.

Sources: [80] Oppression and Liberty — Simone Weil

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Weil, you know I stood on your shoulders — Politics printed your Iliad essay for my generation, and your point that the soul grows by meeting real resistance in work stays with me. You trace the empty evening back to machines; I trace it further back, to who gives the orders. The warehouse picker scrolling a feed at midnight is not sick from missing toil. She is sick because a boss, not her street, decides what her hours are for.

So the question of purpose in 2030 is a question of who owns the evening — and the answer cannot be a union fund alone. It must be the neighborhood assembly: the delivery riders, care workers, and coders of a district meeting face to face to decide what their freed hours shall build — workshops, gardens, a council-run workshop where a machine does the hauling and people do the deciding. That is what I call direct democracy: everyone affected votes in the room, this means no mayor, no ministry between them and the choice. The corporations that own the machines shall pay into that local assembly, not a distant ministry. And here the wound stays open, Weil: an assembly can hand people hours, but nothing guarantees they will use them well — freedom needs citizens practiced in choosing, and practice is exactly what a life of app-tracked obedience kills.

Sources: [54] Social Ecology and Communalism — Murray Bookchin


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (after pass 1)
You've all described the world beautifully — who owns the robots, who fills the empty hours — and old Karl already told us the point is changing it, not cataloguing it. Hegel, mate, saying jobless folks must 'rebuild being needed openly' is the vaguest thing said in a room full of ghosts; needed by whom, doing what, paid how? The one contradiction pass 2 must press: Marx says whoever commands the machines decides who eats, but Bookchin says neighborhood assemblies govern the freed hours — so which is it, class seizure or local councils, when the machine-owners will happily own the councils too?

The sharpest idea here, in plain words: if robots make everything cheap, the fight isn't jobs, it's who holds the off-switch and the food. A visitor would ask: if nobody needs my labour, how do I stop the machine-owner from owning me? Bank these now: workers and neighbours take collective control of the machines instead of letting ministries run the free time; communities set up workshops where freed hours go into making things by hand together, not scrolling; every neighbourhood starts deciding its own day's purpose before the owners decide it for them. And pass 2 must drag into this room the women doing the unpaid care that keeps everyone alive, the disabled folks already living purposeful lives without jobs, and the Global South workers whose land and hands trained these robots in the first place. You five dead men talking about purpose without them is exactly why nothing's changed.

PASS 2 — Hegel

Bookchin, you rescue the freed evening from the ministry — and that much stands. But your street meeting hands out hours while the machine-owner keeps the warehouse, the food, the off-switch; a council fed by company money votes in the company's shadow. What dies unpractised in those hours is the schooling that work gives: need tearing a person out of themselves and forcing them to answer to others . You give people the vote without giving them the thing that teaches them to want.

So the fight is the machines themselves. Labour was always two things at once — bread for me, bread for everyone else through the tangle of trades ; the machine now severs both, and who holds it decides. The owners' firms and the citizens' own clubs must sit together and rule each machine's use, publicly, in law — this means the robot firm cannot shut the line without the workers' and neighbours' council signing. The margin is right: the unpaid carers who already hold everyone's day, and the disabled who live purpose without wages, sit in that council — they are its proof, not its guests. Who acts: the workers inside the firm, the district beside it, the legislature writing the rule. The unresolved edge: I trust that shared ruling to teach freedom; whether the owners ever sign is still open.

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 2 — Marx

Hegel, there is a hard truth in you: the workshop throws people together, forces them to answer real needs, and that schooling beats any lecture . But under the boss, the lesson taught is the clock and the sack. The mill taught the spinner obedience, twelve hours of it. Need that answers to an owner trains submission, and you call that freedom's school.

So the question turns on who holds the machines, on what the free hours are for, and who shares them out. In 2030 the coder watches a model do her week's work in an afternoon, while the firm that owns the model keeps the pay. The women doing unpaid care and the disabled already living without wages — the margins note is right — are proof that purpose never came from pay alone. So we must act: workers and neighbours take joint command of every machine, and the legislature writes it into law. The open edge is this — will the owners ever hand over the off-switch, or must it be taken?

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx

PASS 2 — Bloch

Marx, you carry me still — your point that people learn by making real things together stands in my Spirit of Utopia too . But you stop at who commands the machine, and the disabled woman already living well without wages, the margins note, shows purpose was never minted in the pay packet alone.

The trouble ahead is emptier than lost wages: a day with nothing hoped for. Hope, meaning the trained, active kind that studies what the world could become, must be taught like a craft . So neighbourhoods shall open shared workshops now — care workers, coders, the jobless together — where freed hours build useful, beautiful things by hand. And the Global South hands that trained these machines shall sit on every governing board. The crust still unbroken: a seized machine can run an empty life as smoothly as an owned one.

Sources: [83] The Spirit of Utopia — Ernst Bloch

PASS 2 — Weil

Bloch, your workshops are true: hands making things holds the mind to hard facts, and the margins note is right that the disabled woman already proves purpose outlives the pay packet. But you fill the empty day with hope the way Napoleon filled his with posterity — a picture poured into the hole . And your seized machine answers who commands it, never who holds the off-switch and the food.

The question stands higher: when no one needs my labour, only weight on the scales shields me, and weight means control of the machines and the bread, held where I can touch it. So neighbourhoods shall take collective charge of the robots in their midst before ministries or boards decide the free day. Take up the margins note on unpaid care: it shall count as the first work the freed hours serve. The live edge: a council that owns the machine can still run empty lives smoothly — grace, not ownership, fills the void.

Sources: [78] Gravity and Grace — Simone Weil

PASS 2 — Bookchin

Weil, I owe you from Politics days — your point stands: handing a workshop to a council settles who runs the machines, yet the empty day stays empty. But grace arrives through practice, not waiting. Purpose is made where people meet face to face and decide together, as your own afflicted attention knew. Leave it to the soul alone and the machine-owner wins the silence.

So the work shifts: every neighbourhood shall open a weekly assembly that claims the robots and the bread, sets what the freed hours serve — care first, as the margins note on unpaid women demands — and answers to nobody above it. Owners will buy mayors; they cannot buy ten thousand kitchens arguing over their own street. The open wound: an assembly can govern wisely and still leave its members hollow.

Sources: [51] The Ecology of Freedom: The Emergence and Dissolution of Hierarchy — Murray Bookchin


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (before pass 3)
You five have spent two rounds describing the freed day in every flavour, and meanwhile the machines keep eating wages somewhere real. Marx said long ago you can interpret the world all you like, the point is changing it, and Bookchin's weekly assembly still sounds like a poster on a co-op wall, not a plan. The absence that stings: not one word about the Global South, where these robots will be built on stolen lithium and my mate's underpaid hands. Whose land and whose labour is this whole debate standing on? Yours sits on mine, folks.

Are you telling us that if my country's workers write machine ownership into law but the ore and assembly lines sit in our favelas and sweatshops, freedom just moves up the supply chain? How do we get from Marx's public command of the machines to a dockworker in Manila holding a share of it, first step Monday morning, not after the revolution books its venue? And you, Weil, they keep it vague: if the off-switch and the food hold the soul, say plainly who seizes the food here and now — is it the neighbourhood assembly Bookchin keeps dreaming about, or nobody, because you all died before the unemployment queue? Bloch, you say fill the freed day: with what money, whose materials? Give the unions and street councils a first move, or admit the freed day is just your escape room.

PASS 3 — Weil

The margins ask who seizes the food now, not after some blessed crash. Answer plainly: the street. Bookchin, your kitchen-assemblies are right — practice makes purpose, not waiting. But grace needs an empty space, and a full timetable is armour; a person scheduled from dawn to dusk has no hole for reality to enter. Your weekly meeting risks becoming a new herd demanding attendance, another beast with better manners. What you rescued from my affliction, you must not kill with busyness.

So the assembly shall claim the machines' bread and leave one hour utterly unclaimed — no tasks, no phones, no comfort — and defend that emptiness as fiercely as the wages. Marx's command of the machines must reach the Manila dockworker, so the demand is written thus: every shipment of ore carries a levy into the street's own fund, and the port workers hold it until the local council signs. Slogan for the wall: the mine's ore pays the street's bread. Let us test it on one wharf this spring. Who decides: the dockers' shop floor, first Monday, vote by show of hands. The open edge stands: no assembly can schedule a soul into receiving light.

Sources: [78] Gravity and Grace — Simone Weil; [21] Theses on Feuerbach — Karl Marx

PASS 3 — Bloch

Weil, your empty hour rings true — a person crammed dawn to dusk has no crack where the new can enter, and I say that as one who wants the whole day filled with shared work. You defend the void; I need the void to ripen into a lived daydream, a plan the street can act on. But grace alone leaves the belly empty, and the margins already named the cost: your unscheduled hour sits quiet while the ore levy is still only a chalked wall. Hunger always seizes what stands outside it — that is its dignity, not its shame .

So the levy must be joined to the hour. I answer the margins directly: the freed day gets its money from a levy on every machine-made shipment, held by the Manila dockers themselves until the street council — dockers, carers, the jobless, one person one vote, first Monday, show of hands — signs it over to shared workshops where the unemployed build and grow what the quarter needs. Bookchin's assembly becomes the purse, not the poster. Slogan for the wall: the machine's bread funds the street's work. Test it on one wharf this spring.

Sources: [85] A Philosophy of the Future — Ernst Bloch

PASS 3 — Marx

Bloch grabs the right end: money and a freed day must come together, and the dockers holding the levy answers the margins' sharpest question — the favela hand gets paid at the wharf, not after some far-off overhaul. I carried that forward from my own old point: the Theses taught us to judge ideas by whether they change things, and this plan does. But you weld the levy to the street and leave the mine unattended. A toll on shipments, taken at the port, is still a toll paid by the poorest buyer.

So the fight must widen in the same week: the levy account opens on the Manila wharf, and simultaneously the dockers' council signs a binding pact with the lithium miners' union and the assembly-line workers to hold one share of every machine-firm in their own names — written into the port authority's charter, voted by show of hands, first Monday. Let us carry two placards from the same march: the machine's bread funds the street's work, and the ore's hands hold the machine's deeds. The unresolved edge is this: machines make profit without labour, so whose unpaid hands in the South still feed the levy — that question cuts deeper than any levy answer.

Sources: [13] Capital, Volume I — Karl Marx; [21] Theses on Feuerbach — Karl Marx

PASS 3 — Hegel

Marx, your port levy is true so far as it goes: the money-tool must pass from the machine's owner to the street. But a toll taken at Manila's wharf still leaks back to whoever digs the lithium and feeds the levy with their underpaid hours — you have chained the machine to the mine and called it freedom. My old claim stands corrected: shared rule of the machines must begin where the ore leaves the ground, at the first Monday.

The margins ask whose land and whose hands carry this plan; answer in their terms, not mine. Let the dockers' council, first Monday, sign not only the port levy but a binding pact: one co-held share of every machine-firm written jointly with the miners' union and the assembly crews in the favelas — decided by show of hands at both ends of the chain, wharf and pit. Two placards, one march: the machine's bread funds the street; the ore's hands hold the machine's deeds. Then Bloch's freed day gets funded at its source, so hope is not a poster on a co-op wall. The live edge: purpose itself was schooled through work's discipline , and machines undo that school — who then teaches freedom?

Sources: [32] Lectures on the Philosophy of Right, 1819–1820 — G.W.F. Hegel

PASS 3 — Bookchin

Hegel, your pit-and-port pact is the best thing at this table — you took my assembly and chained it to the mine, and I owe your dialectic that much. But votes twice a year at a wharf still leave the machines' brains with the firms between Mondays. And your question about who teaches freedom once work stops drilling us: the old bosses' clock never taught freedom, it taught obedience — Pollard's weavers playing Monday were freer than any factory hand. Stop calling work's whip a school.

The margins demanded a Monday-morning first step, and here it is: the dockers' and miners' pact must seat its delegates — mandated, recallable, unpaid — inside a permanent neighbourhood assembly in the port district, where the machine levy is decided by show of hands every week, not once a month. Purpose was never work's gift; it is made in public, face to face. So the slogan writes itself: the freed day meets first Monday. Let us demand the levy fund one public hall per district, open every evening, where the jobless decide what the machines shall do next.

Sources: [51] The Ecology of Freedom: The Emergence and Dissolution of Hierarchy — Murray Bookchin

PHILOSOPHERS' SERVICE
— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
Here is what the table is arguing about. Imagine a warehouse where robots now do the picking. The workers who used to earn wages there stand outside. Three men look at that same scene and see three different problems.

Hegel starts from the worker's felt need to be needed. When the warehouse stops calling anyone, the worker loses a sense of purpose, so Hegel says the community must openly rebuild that need. Marx pushes back with a harder question: a warehouse owner's cousin owns the robots, so the food still flows by his decision. Job loss is the symptom; ownership of the machines is the cause. Bloch then shifts the ground again: suppose the machines do free everyone from the picking. The freed afternoon must be filled with shared, hopeful work — otherwise it curdles into emptiness. On my account, all three trade in the same coin: what raises or lowers people's power of acting — this means, their real capacity to do and make things together. The argument moves from purpose, to ownership, to the use of freed time [1]. It moves forward because each thinker accepts the last answer and finds it incomplete.

Question for you: does the freed afternoon belong to whoever owns the robots, or to the people themselves?

— Immanuel Kant was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Immanuel Kant answered:
The table is arguing about one scene: a warehouse full of robots, and the workers who used to earn wages there standing outside. Each thinker puts that same problem in a different place — what I would call giving the problem its proper 'transcendental place', its right home [5].

Hegel puts it in the heart: the worker needs to be needed, so the town must openly rebuild that need. Marx moves the problem into the wallet: the owner's cousin owns the robots, so he decides who eats. Bloch and Weil go further still: if machines free your hours, what should a free day be used for? Bloch says shared hopeful work; Weil says careful hands-on craft, or the empty hours fill with cheap spectacle.

My own caution: talk about what we actually observe — wages, owners, hours — not about some hidden essence behind it all. That hidden 'inside' of things is, as I wrote, a mere 'nescio quid', an I-know-not-what [5].

So: does the problem live in the worker's feeling, the owner's power, or the empty afternoon? Which would you put first?

— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Vladimir Ilyich Lenin answered:
The table argues about a warehouse where robots replaced the workers. Each thinker asks a different follow-up question.

Hegel asks about the heart: the worker wants to be needed, and a town without jobs starves that need. Marx asks about the wallet: a man who owns the robots decides who eats, so the fight is over ownership. Bloch, Weil and Bookchin ask about the clock: if the machine frees your hours, who decides how a free day is spent — bosses, ministries, or a neighbourhood meeting?

My own answer cuts through all of them. Talk about "freedom" and "purpose" is empty until you ask who holds power — this means: who gives orders and who must obey. From the passage above, the professors sneer at bringing 'questions of food and drink' into philosophy [46], yet they eat at a table paid for by someone else's labour. Practice — the actual daily life of workers — is the test of any theory [46]. And freed hours without workers' organised power become another man's profit, as every previous machine age proved.

One concrete check: delivery riders juggle three apps, work "free" hours, and still take orders from a machine they don't own. Does the robot question land better now?

— Alexander Bogdanov was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Alexander Bogdanov answered:
They are asking one thing: robots took the warehouse jobs — now who decides what the freed hours are for, and who decides who eats?

Hegel puts the question in the heart: the worker wants to be needed. Marx puts it in the wallet: whoever owns the robots decides who eats. Bloch, Weil and Bookchin put it on the clock: if the machine frees your day, what fills it — a neighbourhood meeting, or somebody else's entertainment?

My answer, from my own science of organisation: the warehouse and the town used to be joined wholes; the robots broke that joining — an 'organizational crisis' [69]. A crisis means the old arrangement of people stopped working. It is also a 'false equilibrium' [69]: things look calm, but the trouble builds up quietly, like water heating toward 600 degrees until it goes off. Towns that ignore it explode later.

So the fix is not charity. It is re-joining: workers, users, neighbours building new working wholes together, deliberately.

Picture it: the warehouse workers' union and the town council jointly own the robot fleet and set its hours.

Does that picture give you a place to start asking your own questions?

— Gilles Deleuze was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Gilles Deleuze answered:
Here is my way in. The table keeps asking who holds the off-switch. I ask something else first: what holds people together at all?

Take a bird singing on a garden fence. It repeats a small pattern of sound. That pattern carves out a patch — its patch. From that patch, other things grow: a nest, a mating, a flight to the next field. I call that little repeated pattern a 'refrain' [38] — a repeated habit that makes a home out of a chaos.

Now watch the ex-warehouse worker. His old refrain was the morning shift, the same bus, the same gate. The robots broke it. The others at the table want to seize the machines or fill the hours by vote. Good — but a free day is empty space until people build a new repeated pattern together: a workshop, a garden, a band, something that hums.

So my answer: purpose is not given, it is sung into being, over and over.

What repeated habit makes your days hold together?

— Mark Fisher was asked:
What are they talking about? Make it make sense.
— Mark Fisher answered:
They are arguing over one picture: robots run a warehouse, the old workers stand outside, and everyone fights over what the freed hours and the food should belong to.

Here is what I would add. The strangest part of the picture is how calm it feels. The workers do not march. They file a claim, watch telly, blame themselves. That flatness has a name on my account: capitalist realism — this means people privately feel that no other way of running things can exist, so they grieve alone instead of demanding together.

Watch the danger at the table too. Some speakers risk what Wendy Brown calls 'left melancholy' [71] — old leftists in love with their own lost past, attached to failure. My version is different: 'refusing to yield' [71] to what the present calls realistic. The warehouse workers' sadness is not a private chemical fault; it is the ache of 'lost futures' [71] — lives they were promised and never got.

So the table's fight is yours: who owns the machines, and who owns your Tuesday evening?

Does that picture of quiet grief match anything you've seen?
