QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Hegel

Automation replaces labor, removing humans from direct engagement with production. This undermines the educational task of forming citizens capable of participating in the ethical life of the state. Work was once the primary site of education, teaching responsibility, skill, and integration into the social whole.

With machines performing tasks previously reserved for human hands, education must shift focus from technical training to cultivating a conscious participation in the rational ordering of society. The child is no longer shaped by manual labor but must instead learn to recognize their place within a structured ethical community.

PASS 1 — Marx

Hegel, You claim automation removes humans from production, thereby undermining education's role in shaping responsible citizens. But this ignores how automation deepens the contradiction between productive forces and relations of production, forcing education to serve capital’s need for disciplined, docile bodies suited to manage machines—not ethical participants.

Education must now cultivate awareness of the material conditions structuring daily life—how algorithms dictate rhythms, surveillance monitors productivity, and data replaces craftsmanship—as preparation for collective resistance against automated oppression.

PASS 1 — Bloch

Marx, you reduce education to training for discipline under capitalism, missing how automation creates a void where traditional labor roles vanish. This contradiction assumes a stable relation of production, while the Not-Yet-Conscious emerges as the new terrain for human activity.

With necessary work obsolete, education must foster the Not-Yet-Conscious—the latent potential within students to imagine and enact alternative modes of being. Schools should teach children to read the world critically, not submit to it, preparing them for a future where creativity and collaboration supersede routine tasks.

PASS 1 — Weil

Bloch, Your invocation of the Not-Yet-Conscious presumes a subject capable of imagination beyond the void, ignoring that automation has rendered the human worker superfluous—not just in factories, but in every structured domain. This contradicts the reality of necessity: when work vanishes, so does the foundation for disciplined thought.

Education must now confront the emptiness created by automated production—it teaches nothing but submission to machines. Children need not be trained for imagined futures; they require instruction in attending to raw necessity, learning to bear affliction without seeking distraction or escape.

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Weil, your assertion that automation renders the human worker superfluous thereby undermines disciplined thought misunderstands the nature of necessity. Discipline is not bound to labor but to purpose; when work vanishes, it opens space for cultivated attention, not emptiness.

When necessary work disappears, education must cultivate freedom through deliberative engagement. Children learn not submission but responsibility—to attend to necessity as a conscious task, shaping their own potentials amidst abundance.
