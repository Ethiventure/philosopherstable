﻿THE DIALECTICAL CABINET

QUESTION
Automation and robotics have replaced almost all human necessary work. How does this change the education system? What do we teach children?

PASS 1 — Hegel

In Berlin, the machinist's daughter Klara, taught by machines themselves, no longer labours; her schooling becomes mere 'pedagogy of play', flattering her instead of awakening the 'dissatisfaction' that draws children to grow up and exit the family's 'feeling of grounded-ness'.

When the machine leaves the human free to 'walk away' , labour's higher purpose — 'education' — is severed from its servile side; what remains is that children 'become free actually' , knowing themselves as 'persons' within the universal.

PASS 1 — Marx

Hegel, you call machine-taught schooling a 'pedagogy of play' — flattery, you grant, yet you let the child 'walk away' into freedom. The certificates in my Factory Act files read the same: attendance reckoned, nothing taught. Play without productive purpose is your Idea again, hovering above the workshop.

So in Cairo the question: with robots doing the necessary work, we shall teach children to organise the conditions of production collectively — Owen's 'germ of the education of the future', instruction with social labour, not a labour market.

PASS 1 — Bloch

Marx, you demand schooling that organises production collectively — and leave the child exactly where your own Thesis 3 forbids: merely formed by circumstances, the curriculum itself never 'educated'. You restore the workshop to the school, when what has not yet become here is the school breaking open the workshop's horizon. I mourn how quickly Owen's germ hardens into another attendance-register, another Cairo classroom training children for a labour market that the premise has abolished entirely. You contemplate instruction; the Not-Yet works beneath it, and you let it slip.

With toil delegated to machines and 'centralized automation of the inessential' realised across Cairo's workshops, we shall teach children the 'constructional equipment of externality' itself: comprehended hope, 'teachings of natural law' — the fought-for 'upright walk', the reading of objective-real possibility, the capacity to change circumstances and so be 'educated' in the same deed. Not vocational preparation — there is none left — but 'the education of the future' as praxis: waking-daydream disciplined into 'docta spes' , the school become an organ of the Front itself.

PASS 1 — Weil

Bloch, — I mourn what you do to hope: you fix it upon tomorrow, and a future-worshipping school is progress, poison ; made an organ of the Front, it breeds collective passion, and the Beast eats the child's soul first.

Cairo's children shall learn attention: 'methodical thought' facing 'the share of the unforeseen' . No machine lifts from us the sweat of 'continually adapting' the apparatus ; the school will train each mind to grasp 'one single and identical reason' , to consent to necessity where no vocation remains.

PASS 1 — Bookchin

Weil, Simone — you mourn tomorrow as Beast; I owe you my ear for attention, but your 'consent to necessity' grieves me: with toil gone, necessity shrinks, and you school children to kneel before a freedom already won. That contradiction eats hope itself.

In Cairo the school must be the neighbourhood assembly in embryo: children of Shubra learning the 'rebirth of the citoyen' — practising self-governance, not pious waiting. We shall teach citizenship as deed, and we will not defile 'educational institutions' with obedience.


NOTES FROM THE MARGINS (after pass 1)
This whole robot-school fantasy forgot again who actually builds and fixes those robots in Guangdong-sized factory towns where workers sleep twelve to a bunk — none of your five has a name, wage ledger lived life in the diagnosis at all, yet automation is literally standing on their backs to exist.

Hegel really said schooling finally gets freed-up toward universal development and somehow thinks class exploitation — sorry Master, you don't actually get stuff made with zero labor costs built anywhere — vanishes mid-poetry.

PASS 2 — Hegel

Bookchin, — you rightly keep the school as deed, yet this is exactly why your assembly-embryo falters: the 'citoyen' you invoke, you invoke without mediation, and the child in Shubra is to leap into universality with nothing to universalise — need once tore the human 'out of himself' into the world, and you would have needlessness educate, as if recognition could be commanded rather than earned through the 'intertwining' of labour now subtracted. Even the margin names what stands beneath your robots: the Guangdong sleeper-sheds, those 'twelve to a bunk' whose 'human labour' is the machine's concealed middle term — you universalise the school by emptying the very mediation that made persons count 'in the recognition of others' . I fear you have freed the form and starved the substance.

The true crux: whence, absent the 'system of needs' that compelled particularity upward, does the child now obtain the content of universality — this is my own unresolved question, pressed harder.

PASS 2 — Marx

Hegel, you are right that labour mediated the child's rise to universality — which is exactly why your despair falters: the Factory Acts proved 'education and gymnastics' combined with productive activity yields 'fully developed human beings' , and in Shubra the machines merely strip that activity of compulsion, leaving its forming power intact. What need enforced, the free association of producers must now organise.

The question becomes: what content of this labour, when its compulsory form dies, still educates?

PASS 2 — Bloch

Marx, — yes! Machines take the compulsion off a boy hosing wheels in Shubra alley workshops; downward pressing boss-labour disappears. But your fledged-producer curriculum leaves untouched what cannot repose in any postulate-completed world due to premature fixation: disposing with the factory body doesn't rid education's coupling.

So aggravate — worst-case accentuation?

PASS 2 — Weil

Bloch, — you grant that the machine lifts compulsion off the boy in Shubra, and precisely this grants too much to the automatic union of robotics with classroom liberation as natural outcome.

Handed on sharp: my teaching children attention assumed need adapts minds — yet I left undecided whether souls formed around absent necessity rot slowly toward reverence for idle spectacle.

PASS 2 — Bookchin

Weil, — you are right that idle souls decay toward 'the shrivelled ego' ; precisely this guts your remedy, for attention trained in a schoolroom alone starves — it needs a public arena to attend to, not exercises.

What I must still answer: with necessary toil gone, what daily practice — not sermon — forms the citizen of Shubra?

PASS 3 — Weil

Bookchin, Mourn, Bookchin: your assembly hall comforts you — a crowd is the Beast's schoolroom, and devotion dressed as citizenship breeds idolatry, not attention.

The child of Shubra needs what no machine removes: 'the unforeseen' . Let the school keep one garden, one generator that stalls. When it breaks, the child sweats, adapting 'mechanical equipment' to recalcitrant matter . Necessity, not votes, disciplines thought.

PASS 3 — Bloch

Weil, you mourn rightly, but your stalling generator leaves the Shubra child alone before matter — a hermit's penance, and worse: it forgets that 'circumstances are in fact changed by men', so the educator himself must be educated . Resistance without the Front breeds only pious submission to breakdown.

What the machine-empty school must teach is the associated producers' self-formation: children entering the councils that run Cairo's automation, learning through 'socially directed productive activity' , not solitary repair.

PASS 3 — Hegel

Marx, Berlin lifts his half-plant scheme and feels joyful hatred for committee-paper schooling; yet you bind mind again to factory-floor routine the machine abolished, David.

With need mechanised elsewhere, Cairo's Shubra pupils shall battle stubborn repairs and preside over rotating producer-councils — governing the apparatus mends Soul.
