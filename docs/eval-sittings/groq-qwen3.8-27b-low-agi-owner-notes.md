QUESTION
AGI can now do most paid work cheaper than people, and some say humans need jobs to have purpose. So who works, who eats, who decides — and does the system that paid for the machines survive them?

PASS 1 — Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

A coder typing orders in a Berlin office loses her job when a new program does her task cheaper. She finds purpose in the machine, not her own decisions. This breaks the old deal.

When owners remove people from work, society must make a new choice. Who wins depends on the owners, not the rules.

PASS 1 — Karl Marx

You say the machine steals the worker’s purpose. I say the machine steals bread from the worker’s mouth while the owner keeps the profit. The owner does not want purpose; they want a wage cut. This is not a crisis of meaning; it is a crisis of stealing^.

The boss who buys a codebot to fire the coder is not fixing a broken world. They are locking the door while inside^. If one factory replaces workers with machines, the others must do the same to stay alive. This turns human labor into a spare part. The owners of these machines must be taken out of control. Workers must own the tools that shove them out.

PASS 1 — Ernst Bloch

The worker buys a robot to save gold, but loses the chance to shape life. The boss does not seek a flat wage, but a closed cage. This trades living hopes for dead things. The true danger is not money. The true evil is letting tools own us. When we let machines run our thoughts, we stop dreaming of better homes. We become furniture, not builders. The machine is only useful if we steer its power. Owning the robot without the human spirit keeps us asleep.

We must strip the robot of its job-stealing role. We make it a partner. This gives every person free time to learn art, care for kids, and feel deep happiness. This free time is the base for hope. The owners of these factories must hand over control. We build parks, not sweatshops. We choose to live, not just to work. This is the only way to wake up. The struggle is to keep this time sacred, not to lose it to greed. That is the next step for the free.

PASS 1 — Murray Bookchin

You fixate on free time as the reward. That reward is a cage painted gold. In The Ecology of Freedom, I show how separating work from life makes us prisoners. If you surrender control to the few who own the speed, you do not gain a holiday. You gain slavery to a boss who no longer needs your muscle. Hope without open choice is just waiting for scraps.

The real fight is who decides how machines serve the neighborhood. A factory director in Detroit who locks out three thousand workers is not being efficient. He is stealing the town’s voice. We must seize the local assembly. The people there must order the tools to build bridges, not just profit. This is not a dream; it is a demand. The cities must own the means of production today.

PASS 1 — Gilles Deleuze

Bookchin seizes the tool, but who built the tool? You say the city must own the machine. Fine. But the machine does not stop at the city limit. Your local council fights a global flow it cannot hold. The goal is not just local control; it is freeing the flow itself.

The real issue is how desire moves through the grid. We must hack the network, not just own the server. A worker in Bangalore whose code feeds a London bank feels this daily. They must rewrite the rules of access, not just demand a seat at the table. The demand belongs to every node in the web.

— Baruch Spinoza was asked:
What is missing in this conversation?
— Baruch Spinoza answered:
You ask what is missing in the chat about who owns the machines.

First, we need to define the feeling that drives this decision. It is the pulling of the body toward something it wants.

From the passage above, we see that "pain" arises when something we love is missing. This means we feel hurt when our needs are blocked.

Let us look at a warehouse worker in London. She works for hours carrying boxes. Her body feels "absence" of rest. If the owner keeps the profit and not her, her power to move freely shrinks. This is not just theft. It is the breaking of her natural drive to keep herself alive and strong.

The table discussed power over machines. But it missed the daily pain inside the worker’s body caused by that power being denied.

Do you see how physical hunger drives the need for fair pay?

— Immanuel Kant was asked:
What is missing in this conversation?
— Immanuel Kant answered:
You ask what is missing from the talk on machine owners. Hegel looked at who holds the power button. Marx saw the theft of labor. What is missing is the rule that lets you tell true knowledge from a dream. From the passage above, we need specific rules to separate real experience from mere imagination  Without them, we only guess.

Think of a factory floor. A manager claims the robots work for everyone. A worker claims they only make profit for the boss. Which is true? You cannot just listen. You must apply the clear rules of how we know things to see the facts in front of you. This is how you move from arguing about feelings to knowing the actual situation .

Without a clear way to test these claims, everyone just talks past each other. You need a shared standard to check if the data is real or just made up.

Do you see how a rule for truth changes who gets to be believed?

From the passage above, we see that workers need a specific “organisation of revolutionaries”. This is not just a club. It is a disciplined group that plans and acts together. You cannot fix the problem by just knowing it is unfair. You need a plan to seize control.
